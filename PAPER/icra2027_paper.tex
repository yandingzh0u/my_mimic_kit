\begin{abstract}
Physics-based motion tracking is commonly trained with rewards that evaluate
the difference between a simulated character and a reference motion. Learned
differential rewards remove much of the manual weighting required by
handcrafted objectives, but a subtle asymmetry remains: the reward is evaluated
in residual coordinates while the policy is typically conditioned on absolute
reference states. We study whether this coordinate choice alone changes
learning under a finite neural policy and proximal policy optimization. We
compare two one-step command interfaces containing exactly the same
information: an absolute representation
$[\vect{x}_t,\hat{\vect{x}}_t,\hat{\vect{x}}_{t+1}]$ and a residual
representation $[\vect{x}_t,\vect{e}_t,\vect{m}_t]$, where
$\vect{e}_t=\hat{\vect{x}}_t-\vect{x}_t$ and
$\vect{m}_t=\hat{\vect{x}}_{t+1}-\hat{\vect{x}}_t$. The representations are
related by an invertible linear map and therefore induce identical policy
classes. Only the residual representation, however, closes directly under the
reward transition:
$\vect{e}_{t+1}=\vect{e}_t+\vect{m}_t-(\vect{x}_{t+1}-\vect{x}_t)$.

\section{Introduction}
Physics-based character control must coordinate joint pose, velocity, global
motion, and intermittent contacts while respecting dynamics and actuation
limits. A classical tracking reward decomposes this problem into feature-wise
terms,
\begin{equation}
 R_t^{\mathrm{hand}}=\sum_i w_i
 \exp\!\left(-\alpha_i
 \lVert\hat{\vect{q}}_t^i\ominus\vect{q}_t^i\rVert_2^2\right),
 \label{eq:manual}
\end{equation}
where both the component weights $w_i$ and scales $\alpha_i$ are selected by
hand. Such rewards have enabled impressive skills~\cite{peng2018deepmimic}, but
their performance can be sensitive to the aggregation of competing tracking
objectives.

Adversarial Differential Discriminators (ADD) replace the manual aggregation
with a discriminator learned on reference--policy feature differences
\cite{zhang2025add}. The discriminator receives the ideal zero differential as
its positive example and policy tracking differentials as negative examples.
This provides an adaptive nonlinear scalarization while retaining precise,
frame-synchronized tracking. Yet reward design is only one half of the control
interface. The policy must also infer an action from its state and the desired
reference motion. Existing controllers commonly present future target states
in an absolute form, leaving the first layers of the policy to recover the
error coordinates used by the reward.

This paper isolates that interface choice. In the strict causal comparison, we
do not add reference information, change the policy class, shape the reward, or
introduce an auxiliary objective. Instead, we express the same one-step target
information in coordinates that close under the learned reward residual. Each
absolute command can be reconstructed exactly from the residual command and the
current feature state, and vice versa. The official ADD target interface is
retained as an external baseline, but it is not used to establish this
information-equivalence claim because its feature content and lookahead differ.

Our contributions are threefold. First, we formulate absolute and residual
policy commands as an invertible coordinate intervention, separating
optimization effects from additional information or capacity. Second, we
derive an exact residual-transition identity linking the policy command, the
action-realized feature increment, and the next differential reward. Third, we
design a strictly paired evaluation spanning locomotion, aerial rotation,
ground contact, recovery, and long-horizon interaction, with completion
metrics that expose incomplete motions hidden by average tracking error.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Physics-based tracking.}
DeepMimic established a general reinforcement-learning recipe for tracking
individual motion clips with carefully designed pose, velocity, root, and
end-effector rewards~\cite{peng2018deepmimic}. Subsequent systems scale
reference-conditioned control to large motion collections and improve
robustness through architectural, data, and training innovations
\cite{won2020scalable,luo2023phc,tessler2023maskedmimic}. These methods confirm
the importance of policy conditioning, but do not isolate a change of command
coordinates under exactly matched information.

\textbf{Adversarial imitation and learned rewards.}
GAIL casts imitation as adversarial occupancy matching~\cite{ho2016gail}.
AMP learns a reusable motion prior from state transitions and is effective for
motion style~\cite{peng2021amp}; precise synchronization, however, is not its
primary objective. ADD instead discriminates tracking differentials and uses a
single ideal zero vector as the positive example~\cite{zhang2025add}. Our work
retains that learned reward and asks how the corresponding reference command
should be presented to the policy.

\textbf{Relative goals and feedback coordinates.}
Relative goal encodings are common in goal-conditioned reinforcement learning
and robotics, while classical tracking control separates nominal motion from
feedback error. Residual policy learning adds a learned action correction to a
nominal controller~\cite{johannink2019residual}. Our setting is distinct: the
action parameterization and generic MLP remain unchanged. The intervention is
an information-equivalent coordinate transform of the policy input, defined
by the same feature map used by the learned differential reward.

\textbf{Representation and optimization.}
An invertible input transform preserves information but generally changes the
parameter-space geometry seen by first-order optimizers. Natural gradient was
introduced precisely to remove certain parameterization dependencies
\cite{amari1998natural}; ordinary minibatch updates with finite networks do not
have this invariance. Feature centering and whitening exploit this fact for
numerical conditioning, but are agnostic to control semantics. Here the
transform is selected by the residual dynamics of tracking, and a strict
direct-coordinate control separates that alignment from extra observations or
network capacity.

\section{Background}
\subsection{Reference-Conditioned Tracking}
Let $\vect{s}_t$, $\vect{o}_t$, and $\vect{a}_t$ denote the simulator state,
policy observation, and action. The next state is sampled from
$p(\vect{s}_{t+1}\mid\vect{s}_t,\vect{a}_t)$. A synchronized reference provides
$\hat{\vect{s}}_t$ and $\hat{\vect{s}}_{t+1}$. A common feature map
$\phi$ extracts root pose, body configuration, and velocities:
\begin{equation}
 \vect{x}_t=\phi(\vect{s}_t),\quad
 \hat{\vect{x}}_t=\phi(\hat{\vect{s}}_t),\quad
 \hat{\vect{x}}_{t+1}=\phi(\hat{\vect{s}}_{t+1}).
 \label{eq:features}
\end{equation}
Rotations are represented in a fixed Euclidean embedding, so all differences
below are taken in a shared feature chart.

\subsection{Learned Differential Reward}
The tracking differential is
\begin{equation}
 \vect{\Delta}_t=\phi(\hat{\vect{s}}_t)\ominus\phi(\vect{s}_t).
\end{equation}
Following ADD~\cite{zhang2025add}, let $d_\psi$ denote the discriminator logit
and $p_\psi=\sigma(d_\psi)$ its probability. The discriminator separates
$\vect{\Delta}=\vect{0}$ from policy differentials using
\begin{align}
 \mathcal{L}_D ={}&\operatorname{softplus}[-d_\psi(\vect{0})]
 +\mathbb{E}_{\vect{s}_t\sim\pi}\operatorname{softplus}[d_\psi(\vect{\Delta}_t)]\nonumber\\
 &+\lambda_{\mathrm{GP}}\mathbb{E}_{\vect{s}_t\sim\pi}
 \lVert\nabla_{\vect{\Delta}}d_\psi(\vect{\Delta}_t)\rVert_2^2 .
 \label{eq:disc}
\end{align}
The policy reward is
\begin{equation}
 R_t^{\mathrm{diff}}=\beta\operatorname{softplus}
 [d_\psi(\vect{\Delta}_t)]
 =-\beta\log[1-p_\psi(\vect{\Delta}_t)].
 \label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
Both compared command interfaces share this discriminator, replay,
regularization, reward scale, and update schedule.

\section{Residual-Coordinate Policy Commands}
\subsection{Two Information-Matched Interfaces}
The direct-target interface gives the policy
\begin{equation}
 \vect{z}^{\mathrm{tar}}_t=
 [\vect{x}_t,\hat{\vect{x}}_t,\hat{\vect{x}}_{t+1}],
 \label{eq:absolute}
\end{equation}
whereas the residual interface defines
\begin{equation}
 \vect{e}_t=\hat{\vect{x}}_t-\vect{x}_t,\qquad
 \vect{m}_t=\hat{\vect{x}}_{t+1}-\hat{\vect{x}}_t,
 \label{eq:em}
\end{equation}
and provides
\begin{equation}
 \vect{z}^{\mathrm{res}}_t=[\vect{x}_t,\vect{e}_t,\vect{m}_t].
 \label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
Both interfaces use one-step lookahead, the same $\phi$, and the same number of
features. The actor remains a generic Gaussian MLP
$\pi_\theta(\vect{a}_t\mid\vect{o}_t,\vect{z}_t)$.

\subsection{Information Equivalence}
\textbf{Proposition 1.} Conditioned on $\vect{x}_t$, the direct-target and
residual interfaces are affine bijections. In their concatenated form they are
related by the linear transform
\begin{equation}
 \vect{z}^{\mathrm{res}}_t=
 \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
 I&0&0\\[-1mm]-I&I&0\\[-1mm]0&-I&I
 \end{bmatrix}}_{T}\vect{z}^{\mathrm{tar}}_t,\qquad \det(T)=1.
 \label{eq:T}
\end{equation}
The inverse is
$\hat{\vect{x}}_t=\vect{x}_t+\vect{e}_t$ and
$\hat{\vect{x}}_{t+1}=\vect{x}_t+\vect{e}_t+\vect{m}_t$.

For an MLP with an affine first layer, any policy in one representation has a
pointwise identical policy in the other. Let
$\bar{T}=\operatorname{diag}(I_{\vect{o}},T)$. Given first-layer weights
$W_{\mathrm{tar}}$, choose
$W_{\mathrm{res}}=W_{\mathrm{tar}}\bar{T}^{-1}$; all subsequent activations and
the action distribution are then identical. Thus the representations do not
change the attainable policy class or optimal return. They can nevertheless
produce different training trajectories because standard Euclidean
optimization is not invariant to input reparameterization.

\emph{Proof.} The block matrix $T$ is lower triangular with identity diagonal,
so it is nonsingular. Its inverse reconstructs
$\hat{\vect{x}}_t=\vect{x}_t+\vect{e}_t$ and
$\hat{\vect{x}}_{t+1}=\vect{x}_t+\vect{e}_t+\vect{m}_t$. For any affine first
layer $h=W_{\mathrm{tar}}[\vect{o};\vect{z}^{\mathrm{tar}}]+b$, substitution of
$\vect{z}^{\mathrm{tar}}=T^{-1}\vect{z}^{\mathrm{res}}$ yields the same $h$ with
$W_{\mathrm{res}}=W_{\mathrm{tar}}\bar T^{-1}$. Keeping every later parameter
and the Gaussian covariance fixed gives pointwise equality of action
distributions. Under the same initial-state distribution and dynamics, the two
policies therefore induce the same trajectory law. $\square$

\subsection{Shared Standardization}
A matched comparison must preserve the bijection after preprocessing. We use a
fixed demonstration-derived diagonal scale $S$ for every policy-command
$\phi$ block:
\begin{equation}
 \bar{\vect{e}}_t=S^{-1}\vect{e}_t,\quad
 \bar{\vect{m}}_t=S^{-1}\vect{m}_t,
 \label{eq:scale}
\end{equation}
and apply the same $S$ and centering convention to the absolute features.
Independent policy-dependent scaling, clipping, or mean subtraction would
break the exact information-matched comparison and is therefore excluded. This
shared command scale should not be confused with ADD's stock differential
normalizer inside $d_\psi$: the two modules share the raw $\phi$ chart, while
their numerical normalizers remain separate and identical across paired runs.

\subsection{Multi-Step Commands}
The construction extends directly to multiple future frames. For an $H$-step
target, define
\begin{equation}
 \vect{m}_{t,k}=\hat{\vect{x}}_{t+k+1}-\hat{\vect{x}}_{t+k},
 \qquad k=0,\ldots,H-1.
 \label{eq:multistep}
\end{equation}
The two commands
\begin{align}
 \vect{z}_{t,H}^{\mathrm{tar}}
  &=[\vect{x}_t,\hat{\vect{x}}_t,\ldots,\hat{\vect{x}}_{t+H}],\\
 \vect{z}_{t,H}^{\mathrm{res}}
  &=[\vect{x}_t,\vect{e}_t,\vect{m}_{t,0},\ldots,\vect{m}_{t,H-1}]
 \label{eq:multiz}
\end{align}
remain information equivalent because
$\hat{\vect{x}}_{t+k}=\vect{x}_t+\vect{e}_t+
\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\vect{m}_{t,j}$. The transform is block lower triangular with
identity diagonal. We use $H=1$ in the primary study to isolate the smallest
closed command and avoid conflating coordinates with extra lookahead. A matched
$H=3$ experiment tests whether the effect persists at the horizon commonly
used by an official target interface. Later increments do not change the
one-step closure; they expose the desired motion beyond the next transition in
the same chart.

\subsection{Residual Closure}
Let the realized feature increment be
$\vect{\delta}_t=\vect{x}_{t+1}-\vect{x}_t$. Expanding the next residual gives
\begin{align}
 \vect{e}_{t+1}
 &=\hat{\vect{x}}_{t+1}-\vect{x}_{t+1}\nonumber\\
 &=\vect{e}_t+\vect{m}_t-\vect{\delta}_t.
 \label{eq:closure}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:closure} is the central structural property: the current
error and desired reference increment supplied to the policy, the physical
response produced by its action, and the next residual evaluated by the reward
all inhabit the same coordinates. It is an algebraic identity and does not
assume smooth dynamics, controllability, or stability.

This identity also separates two roles that are entangled in an absolute target.
The residual $\vect{e}_t$ specifies the correction required by the current
tracking state, while $\vect{m}_t$ specifies how the target itself moves during
the coming control interval. When $\vect{e}_t=0$, the policy still receives the
nonzero command needed to advance along the reference. When $\vect{m}_t=0$, it
receives a pure regulation problem. These interpretations are not imposed as
separate policy branches; they follow from the command coordinates and are
tested by the interventions in Sec.~VII.

\subsection{A Finite-Network Inductive Bias}
The bijection rules out an information or expressivity advantage, but it does
not imply equal parameter complexity. Consider a locally linear response
$\vect{u}=K_e\vect{e}+K_m\vect{m}$. In residual coordinates its first-layer
blocks are $[0,K_e,K_m]$. Written against the absolute variables, the identical
response has blocks
\begin{equation}
 [-K_e,\;K_e-K_m,\;K_m].
 \label{eq:linearweights}
\end{equation}
In squared parameter norm, the absolute representation carries the additional
term $\lVert K_e-K_m\rVert_F^2$. This example is not a model of
the nonlinear contact controller, nor a proof that one chart always learns
faster; the actor optimizer need not explicitly penalize this norm. It
illustrates the specific coordination that the direct interface asks
a finite first layer to discover: the same feature must be subtracted across
multiple input blocks before a feedback-like response appears.

\subsection{Optimization Geometry}
Although $T$ preserves information, it does not preserve the Euclidean metric
used by gradient descent. For function-matched policies on the same batch, the
first-layer gradients satisfy
\begin{equation}
 G_{\mathrm{res}}=G_{\mathrm{tar}}\bar{T}^{\top}.
 \label{eq:grad}
\end{equation}
Mapping one residual-coordinate SGD step back into absolute parameters inserts
the fixed metric $\bar{T}^{\top}\bar{T}$. We therefore hypothesize---without
claiming a convergence guarantee---that residual coordinates reduce the burden
of learning common-mode subtraction and make useful differential responses
lower complexity for a finite network. Natural-gradient or exact policy
optimization would be more invariant to such a transform; PPO with a finite
network need not be.

\section{Experimental Design}
\subsection{Research Questions and Controls}
We ask: (Q1) Does residual conditioning improve final tracking and full-motion
completion under strictly matched information? (Q2) Is the effect consistent
across seeds and motions? (Q3) Do targeted coordinate ablations support the
residual-closure interpretation?

The primary causal comparison changes only
\eqref{eq:absolute} versus \eqref{eq:residual}. Environment count, physics
samples, episode horizon, phase reset, termination, contact settings, actor and
critic widths, optimizer, learning rates, discriminator architecture, replay,
normalization budget, and reward scale are identical. Runs use paired random
seeds and the final pre-specified checkpoint. Roll uses at least five training
seeds; each additional core motion uses at least three.

\subsection{Motions and Metrics}
The benchmark suite spans Run, Jump, Backflip, Crawl, Getup Facedown, SpinKick,
and Climb. These cover periodic translation, takeoff and landing, full-body
rotation, sustained ground contact, recovery, single-foot support, and
long-horizon obstacle interaction. Roll is retained as a mechanism task for an
observed incomplete-motion failure; Sideflip is a confirmatory dynamic task and
is reported only after matched training completes.

We retain the standard position and degree-of-freedom (DoF) velocity tracking
errors used by ADD~\cite{zhang2025add}. Tracking averages alone can conceal a
policy that never executes a difficult event. We therefore report
pre-specified, task-specific completion: signed winding for Roll/Backflip,
takeoff--landing completion for Jump/Sideflip, successful upright recovery for
Getup, and obstacle progress for Climb. Training seed, rather than episode, is
the statistical unit; tables report every seed, mean $\pm$ standard deviation,
95\% intervals, and paired effect sizes.

\subsection{Baselines and Ablations}
We compare with a handcrafted DeepMimic-style reward, AMP, and ADD using its
official target interface. The internal causal controls are the matched
direct-target and residual-coordinate representations. Ablations retain the
input dimension through zero padding: error-only $[\vect{x},\vect{e},0]$,
motion-only $[\vect{x},0,\vect{m}]$, and the non-invertible summed command
$[\vect{x},\vect{e}+\vect{m},0]$. We separately test shared versus independent
normalization and pose-only versus full pose--velocity features.

\subsection{Implementation and Evaluation Protocol}
The simulated character has 28 actuated DoFs. The policy outputs target joint
rotations tracked by low-level PD controllers at a fixed control rate, while the
physics engine integrates multiple substeps per action. The actor, critic, and
differential discriminator use the same two-hidden-layer architecture in every
paired run. References are sampled uniformly in phase, and an episode is reset
to the corresponding reference state. Target phases then advance at the clip's
recorded rate. Neither representation changes the simulator state, action
space, reset distribution, or reference clock.

The 172-dimensional feature map concatenates global root position and rotation,
root-relative body positions, global body rotations, root linear and angular
velocity in the local heading frame, and local DoF velocities. Rotations use a
continuous Euclidean embedding before subtraction. Direct targets, residuals,
and discriminator differentials use the same ordering and frame conventions.
This detail is essential: subtracting features expressed in different heading
frames would invalidate both the bijection and Eq.~\eqref{eq:closure}. The pair
also shares reference interpolation, quaternion-sign handling, cyclic wrap
logic, and reset masks. Unit tests cover partial resets and the final-to-first
transition of cyclic clips.

The direct and residual inputs are constructed from the same cached feature

behavioral evaluations use deterministic mean actions, held-out phase samples,
and the last training checkpoint; no task-completion metric enters training or
checkpoint selection.

\subsection{Evaluation Measures}
Following the published motion-tracking protocol, the primary geometric error
combines root displacement with root-relative joint positions:
\begin{align}
 E_t^{\mathrm{pos}}=\frac{1}{N_J+1}\bigg(&\lVert
 \hat{\vect{p}}_t^0-\vect{p}_t^0\rVert_2 +
 \sum_{j=1}^{N_J}\lVert(\hat{\vect{p}}_t^j-\hat{\vect{p}}_t^0)
 -(\vect{p}_t^j-\vect{p}_t^0)\rVert_2\bigg).
 \label{eq:posmetric}
\end{align}
We also report mean DoF-velocity error
$E_t^{\mathrm{vel}}=N_q^{-1}\sum_j|\hat{\dot q}_{t,j}-\dot q_{t,j}|$.
Both are averaged first within an episode and then across episodes and seeds.

Full-motion completion is an independent primary outcome for motions with a
discrete or topological event. For a rotation, our existing evaluator uses the
vector-valued integrated root angular velocities
$\vect{\Omega}=\sum_t\vect{\omega}_t\Delta t$ and
$\hat{\vect{\Omega}}=\sum_t\hat{\vect{\omega}}_t\Delta t$:
\begin{equation}
 W=\frac{\langle\vect{\Omega},\hat{\vect{\Omega}}\rangle}
 {\lVert\hat{\vect{\Omega}}\rVert_2^2},\qquad
 \text{incomplete if }W<0.5.
 \label{eq:winding}
\end{equation}
We additionally report the root displacement ratio and survival duration;
success requires the correct direction and a pre-specified interval around the
reference value. Jump requires takeoff followed by a stable landing, Getup requires
upright recovery maintained for a fixed dwell time, and Climb requires the root
and feet to clear the final obstacle region. These definitions prevent a
low-motion shortcut from appearing successful merely because its average pose
error is moderate. Thresholds are fixed once for each motion and shared by all
methods.

\subsection{Function-Matched Optimization Test}
Randomly initialized direct and residual networks need not represent the same

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{What the Coordinate Change Does}
The proposed interface should be interpreted as a control representation, not
a new reward or policy architecture. It supplies the policy with the current
tracking error and reference increment in the same feature chart in which the
next outcome is scored. Proposition~1 rules out additional information and
expressive capacity as explanations in the idealized matched setting.
Equation~\eqref{eq:closure} then provides a concrete reason the chart is useful:
the policy does not need to discover the subtraction that connects absolute
targets to the reward residual before it can represent differential feedback.

This distinction matters for learned rewards. A discriminator may provide a
useful scalar objective while policy optimization still fails to discover a
controller that executes the full motion. Changing the command chart can alter
the implicit metric and inductive bias of a finite optimizer without changing
which policies are representable or which trajectory is globally optimal.
The contribution is therefore a controlled study of the policy--reward
interface, rather than a claim that residual coordinates solve exploration in
general.

The size of a behavioral change need not match the apparent size of the code
change. Contact-rich control is learned through a long sequence of coupled
on-policy updates. A coordinate transform that changes which first-layer
responses are easy to represent can alter the early state distribution; that
distribution changes the discriminator's negative samples, the critic targets,
and all later policy gradients. The intervention is small at the interface but
its training effect is path dependent. Proposition~1 prevents attributing that
effect to a more expressive optimal policy, while the function-matched test
separates it from a different random initial function.

\subsection{Limitations}
Residual closure is not a stability theorem. The root is underactuated,
contacts are hybrid and nonsmooth, and a one-step reference increment may be
physically infeasible from an off-reference state. A generic MLP can freely mix
$\vect{e}_t$ and $\vect{m}_t$; the terms are feedback- and feedforward-aligned
coordinates, not identifiable controller branches. Rotation differences are
chords in the chosen embedding, not general Lie-algebra tangents. The policy
remains reference conditioned and does not define a reference-blind autonomous
skill. Finally, evidence from single-motion or single-seed studies cannot
establish a general optimization advantage; the confirmatory suite and strict
paired protocol are essential.

The equivalence theorem is conditional on access to $\vect{x}_t$ and on
invertible preprocessing. It does not apply to an official target interface
that omits velocity features or uses a different lookahead. Nor does it imply
that every feature coordinate is controllable in one step: root motion is
underactuated and contact impulses can dominate the immediate response. Our
completion definitions are external to the learned reward, but remain
task-specific evaluation choices and must be fixed before inspecting method
outcomes. Finally, the present study uses one simulated humanoid and
motion-capture references. Transfer to different morphologies, noisy state
estimation, actuation delay, and hardware requires separate validation.

\subsection{Implications for Robot Learning}
Many robot-learning systems specify goals in one representation but evaluate
behavior in another. The present analysis suggests a simple design question:
can the command be expressed in coordinates that close under the task residual
without adding information? This principle may apply to end-effector tracking,
legged-locomotion commands, visual servoing, and model-predictive residuals.
Those extensions require task-specific feature charts and remain future work.

\subsection{Computational Cost and Reproducibility}
The command construction uses only feature subtraction and fixed diagonal
scaling. It adds no learned parameters and requires no additional simulator
steps. We nevertheless report environment throughput, policy latency, and peak
memory for both representations. The release will include the feature ordering,
frozen scales, inverse-transform unit test, paired seed list, final-checkpoint

\section{Conclusion}
We study the coordinate interface between a reference-conditioned policy and a
learned differential tracking reward. Absolute and residual one-step commands
contain exactly the same information and support the same policy class, but the
residual command obeys an exact post-action closure identity with the reward.
This yields a minimal intervention for testing how representation shapes finite
policy optimization.
