\begin{abstract}
Physics-based humanoid imitation must satisfy several heterogeneous
objectives at once: global root motion, articulated pose, and velocity all
have to agree with a synchronized reference. Handcrafted tracking rewards
combine these objectives with weights and scales chosen per motion.
Learned differential rewards remove the weights, but they keep the
discriminator from collapsing onto its single positive example with a
gradient penalty whose strength must again be chosen per motion, because
that one coefficient decides both how sharp the discriminator may be and
how far typical policy residuals are pushed from the ideal. We introduce
DARE, a differential adversarial reward that fixes these two properties
separately, with no motion-specific coefficient. The geometry of the critic is
fixed by construction: every semantic block of the differential enters
through its own spectrally normalized map, so objectives of very
different dimension receive equal capacity and equal gain, and the score
change relative to the unique zero-error anchor is bounded by the size of
the residual. The scale of the critic is fixed once, in closed form, from
the reward transform: the balanced adversarial loss places the anchor and
the typical policy residual symmetrically around zero, and DARE rescales
the critic so that this separation equals the width of the window over
which the reward transform responds to the critic's score. The rule is
shared by every motion; only the measured separation depends on the
data. We evaluate DARE with one configuration
on six humanoid skills with distinct dynamical regimes---Roll, Climb,
Backflip, Crawl, Get-Up, and Spin Kick---\todo{one-sentence quantitative
summary from the formal runs}, and deploy the resulting controllers on
\todo{robot platform}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Physics-based humanoid control must reproduce a prescribed reference
motion under dynamics, actuation limits, and intermittent contact.
Whether an imitation is accurate is not a single geometric question. The
root has to travel and turn as the reference does, the articulated body
has to take the same shape, joint and root velocities have to agree, and
contacts have to occur at the right moment. These objectives are measured
in different units, evolve on different time scales, and interact through
the dynamics, so motion imitation is a multi-objective problem whose
objectives cannot be pursued independently.

The standard response is to define a tracking term for each error and to
combine the terms by hand. A representative scalar reward is
\begin{equation}
 r_t^{\mathrm{hand}}
 =\sum_{g=1}^{G} w_g
 \exp\!\Bigl[-\alpha_g\bigl\|\phi_g(\hat{s}_t)-\phi_g(s_t)\bigr\|_2^2\Bigr],
 \label{eq:hand}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_g$ extracts the $g$-th motion feature---joint rotations, root
position, velocities, end-effector positions---from the simulated state
$s_t$ and the synchronized reference state $\hat{s}_t$. The weights $w_g$
decide how objectives exchange against one another, and the scales
$\alpha_g$ fix the numerical range of each error. When the motion changes,
typical error magnitudes, learning difficulty, and dynamical roles change
with it, so coefficients that serve one skill need not serve the next, and
reward construction is repeated for every new motion.

Adversarial imitation offers a different route. Instead of declaring how
many meters of root error are worth how many radians of joint error, a
discriminator learns a scalar imitation signal from data. Motion priors
such as AMP do this at the level of short state transitions and reward
motion that resembles the dataset \cite{peng2021amp}. Adversarial
differential discriminators make the tracking case precise: the
discriminator receives the reference--policy differential and classifies
whether it is the ideal zero vector, so the aggregation of tracking
objectives is learned rather than specified \cite{zhang2025add}. The
weights and scales of~\eqref{eq:hand} disappear.

One coefficient remains, and it inherits the tuning burden. The
differential formulation has exactly one positive example, the origin,
and the optimal discriminator for a single positive point is a delta
function: it scores the origin as ideal and every other differential as
equally hopeless, and the policy receives no gradient. The discriminator
must therefore be kept from becoming arbitrarily sharp, and the standard
tool is a gradient penalty of strength $\lambda$. The coefficient is
delicate in both directions, because the policy's reward is a saturating
function of the discriminator's score. If $\lambda$ is too small, the
discriminator sharpens until the residuals the policy actually produces
are all scored far below the ideal; the reward saturates at zero across
the states the policy visits, and neither staying alive nor tracking
slightly better is rewarded. If $\lambda$ is too large, the discriminator is smoothed until
it can no longer separate residuals that differ in the details a skill
depends on; the reward is nearly the same on every state, survival is
rewarded, and tracking is not. Where the usable range lies depends on the
residual distribution of the motion being learned, and the published
configurations accordingly use different penalty strengths for different
clips and characters. The coefficient must be found again for every skill
because it controls two things at once: how steep the discriminator may
be anywhere, which is a property of its geometry, and how far the typical
residual is pushed from the ideal, which is a property of its scale.

DARE fixes each of these with no motion-specific coefficient. The
geometry is fixed by construction, using two structural facts of the
differential: perfect imitation is always the same point, $\Delta=0$,
whatever the motion or phase---we call this point the anchor---and the
differential is a direct sum of
semantic blocks---root position, root rotation, body position, body
rotation, root velocities, joint velocities---which are the objectives of
the tracking problem. Differential discriminators were conceived for
general multi-objective optimization, where each objective is one scalar
loss and the differential has one entry per objective, so all objectives
enter on equal footing. The tracking instance replaces each entry by the
raw coordinates of its objective, and the footing is lost: an
$84$-dimensional body-rotation block competes inside one dense layer with
a $3$-dimensional root-position block and wins by coordinate count. DARE
restores the footing by mapping each block through its own spectrally
normalized map to an equal-width latent---a learned, vector-valued
generalization of the per-objective scalar loss---before a shared
Lipschitz critic mixes the latents; the resulting critic changes its
score, relative to the anchor, by no more than the size of the residual,
for every motion and with no coefficient.

The scale is fixed by one calibration rather than by construction,
because a Lipschitz bound says how steep a critic may be and not how steep
it is. The balanced adversarial loss places the anchor score and the
typical residual score symmetrically about zero, at $\pm M/2$ for a gap
$M$ that the loss drives as wide as the function class and the data
allow. The saturating reward transform, however, responds to the score
only inside a window of fixed width, and the two failure modes above are
$M$ overshooting or undershooting that window. DARE measures $M$ once,
when the input units become fixed, and rescales the critic so that anchor
and typical residual sit at the two ends of the window. The rule is the same for every
motion; only the measured $M$, like the input normalization it follows,
depends on the data.

The contributions are as follows. First, we identify that the
regularization coefficient of differential adversarial rewards conflates
critic geometry with critic scale, and we show that this is why it must
be re-tuned per motion. Second, we introduce DARE, whose factorized,
spectrally normalized critic fixes the geometry by construction and whose
one-shot anchor calibration fixes the scale in closed form from the reward
transform, so that no reward or regularization coefficient is specified
per motion. Third, we evaluate DARE with one configuration across six
heterogeneous skills and execute the resulting controllers on
\todo{robot platform}.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Physics-based humanoid imitation.} DeepMimic reproduces diverse
motion-capture clips by combining pose, velocity, root, and end-effector
tracking kernels with user-chosen coefficients \cite{peng2018deepmimic};
later systems scale tracking through architecture and data
\cite{won2020scadiver,luo2023phc,tessler2023maskedmimic,peng2018sfv}
while their imitation objectives still rest on manual scales and weights.
Our concern is the objective itself: a reward that can be reused across
motions.

\textbf{Adversarial imitation and learned rewards.} GAIL casts imitation
as adversarial occupancy matching \cite{ho2016gail}, and AMP adapts this
idea to physics-based characters by discriminating short state
transitions \cite{peng2021amp}; a reward that scores ``looking like the
dataset'' suits stylized behavior but can accept a plausible yet
incomplete motion. Differential rewards hand the reference--policy error
itself to a discriminator \cite{zhang2025add}: ADD composes the tracking
objectives into one differential, treats the zero vector as the sole
positive example, and learns a nonlinear aggregation without component
weights, stabilized by a gradient penalty whose strength is set per
motion. DARE continues this program and removes that coefficient.

\textbf{Stable adversarial learning.} Gradient penalties, Lipschitz
constraints, and spectral parameterization keep adversarial critics from
becoming either brittle or saturated
\cite{gulrajani2017improved,miyato2018spectral,mescheder2018convergence};
for losses other than the Wasserstein loss, what such a constraint
controls is the range of scores the loss operates on, and the scale of
that range determines whether training succeeds \cite{qin2020lipschitz}.
DARE uses the constraint for geometry alone and fixes the score range
separately.

\textbf{Multi-objective optimization.} Weighted sums, Pareto methods,
adaptive loss balancing, and multi-critic RL all address competing
objectives and typically require explicit preferences or multiple
objective gradients
\cite{sener2018multitask,kendall2018multi,abdolmaleki2020distributional};
differential adversarial learning instead learns a nonlinear
scalarization from data, and DARE performs it with one critic and one
configuration for every motion.

\section{Background}
\subsection{Reference-Conditioned Humanoid Control}
Let $s_t$ be the simulated state at time $t$, $\hat{s}_t$ the
synchronized reference state, and $a_t$ the action, a vector of desired
joint positions that a proportional--derivative servo converts into
torques at the control frequency. A policy $\pi_\varphi$ observes
proprioception together with the next few reference frames and must close
the loop under contact and underactuation; episodes begin at a random
reference phase, so the policy has to recover the clip from any point. The
policy maximizes
the discounted return of a reward $r_t$ with PPO
\cite{schulman2017ppo,schulman2016gae}; Section~\ref{sec:dare} constructs
$r_t$.

\subsection{Motion Tracking as Multi-Objective Optimization}
A motion descriptor decomposes a state into $G$ feature groups
$\phi_g:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_g}$, each describing one physical
aspect of the motion: global root position, root rotation, root-relative
body positions, body rotations, root linear and angular velocity, and
joint velocity. A handcrafted reward first compresses each group into a
distance $e_{g,t}=\|\phi_g(\hat{s}_t)-\phi_g(s_t)\|_2$ and then applies
the fixed map~\eqref{eq:hand}. The widely used DeepMimic reward does this
with five terms whose weights $(0.5,0.1,0.15,0.1,0.15)$ and scales
$(0.25,0.01,5,1,10)$ were selected by hand, together with fourteen
joint-specific weights inside the pose and velocity terms
\cite{peng2018deepmimic,zhang2025add}: twenty-four coefficients, each
encoding an assumption about how large an error of one kind is worth
relative to another, frozen before the residual distribution of any
policy is seen. A learned scalarization can instead adapt the trade-off
to the residuals the current policy actually produces.

\subsection{Learned Differential Objectives}
\label{sec:learned}
Differential adversarial learning provides that scalarization
\cite{zhang2025add}. In its general form, $n$ objectives with
non-negative losses $l^1,\ldots,l^n$ are collected into a differential
vector $\Delta=(l^1,\ldots,l^n)$ that records the gap between ideal and
actual performance on every objective. The ideal solution is the single
point $\Delta=0$. A discriminator $D(\Delta)$ trained to separate this
point from the differentials of the current model is a learned, nonlinear
aggregation of the objectives, and its gradient $\partial D/\partial l^i$
is the weight the aggregation currently assigns to objective $i$; that
weight moves during training toward the objectives on which the model
still falls short. For synchronized motion imitation the same
construction is applied to the feature differential
$\phi(\hat{s}_t)-\phi(s_t)$. Perfect tracking maps every pair onto one
origin, so the positive class is again a single known point, and the
differential retains information about \emph{how} the current motion
departs from the reference rather than collapsing it into a state
occupancy. The policy is rewarded at each step with
$-\log(1-D(\Delta_t))$, as in AMP: large when the discriminator finds the
residual $\Delta_t$ close to ideal, and vanishing when it does not.

A single positive point has a consequence. Without regularization the
optimal discriminator is a delta function at the origin, which scores
every policy residual as equally hopeless, so the reward vanishes on
every state the policy visits and provides no gradient. The
discriminator is therefore trained with a gradient penalty
$\lambda\,\mathbb{E}_{\Delta\sim q_\pi}\|\nabla_\Delta D(\Delta)\|_2^2$
added to the classification objective, where $q_\pi$ denotes the
distribution of policy residuals. The penalty acts on sampled residuals,
so its effect depends on where those residuals lie, and a fixed $\lambda$
yields a differently shaped discriminator on every motion. Small $\lambda$
lets the discriminator approach the delta function and saturate the
reward; large $\lambda$ smooths it until residuals that differ in
skill-relevant detail receive the same score. The published
configurations bear this out: $\lambda$ is set to $0.1$ for the Climb
clip and to $1$ for the other humanoid clips, and to different values
again for a biped, a quadruped, and a planar walker, while removing the
penalty altogether fails \cite{zhang2025add}. How those values were
chosen is not reported; what they document is that no single value served
all settings. Locating the window between the two failures is the tuning
that the learned aggregation was meant to remove; the next section shows
that $\lambda$ is asked to do two separable jobs and does each without
it.

\section{DARE: Differential Adversarial Reward Equalization}
\label{sec:dare}
\subsection{Learned Differential Reward}
\label{sec:reward}
The distances $e_{g,t}$ are convenient for a weighted sum, but they
discard the signed structure of the residual. Two policies can share the
same root-position distance while drifting in opposite directions, and
only one of them remains compatible with the next contact. DARE therefore
keeps the differential itself. For each group $g$, a normalizer
$\mathcal{N}_g=\operatorname{diag}(\nu_g)^{-1}$ rescales every coordinate
by its mean absolute value $\nu_g$, estimated from the residuals of the
first $10^{8}$ training samples and then fixed, as is standard for
adversarial motion rewards. The normalizer has no offset, so it removes
unit disparity between meters, rotation coordinates, and velocities while
leaving the origin fixed; rotations use a tangent--normal embedding so
that differences are Euclidean. The group-wise residual, the full
differential, and the ideal point are
\begin{equation}
 \Delta_{g,t}=\mathcal{N}_g\bigl(\phi_g(\hat{s}_t)-\phi_g(s_t)\bigr),
 \qquad
 \Delta_t=\bigoplus_{g=1}^{G}\Delta_{g,t},
 \qquad
 \Delta^{+}=0 .
 \label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}
The vector $\Delta_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$, $d=\sum_g d_g$, retains error
direction and cross-coordinate relationships instead of $G$ distances, and every
perfectly synchronized pair maps to the same origin regardless of the
motion or its phase. Differentials produced by the current policy,
together with recently visited differentials kept in a replay buffer,
form the distribution $q_\pi$ of non-ideal residuals.

The remaining question is how to turn $\Delta$ into a scalar the policy
can maximize. Rather than assigning a price to each block, we ask a critic
to recognize the ideal point. Let
$D_\theta(\Delta)=\sigma\bigl(f_\theta(\Delta)\bigr)$ be the probability
that a differential is ideal, where $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid and
$f_\theta:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}$ is a scalar score. The origin is the
positive class and residuals drawn from $q_\pi$ are the negative class, so
the critic plays the usual two-player game,
\begin{equation}
 \max_{\theta}\;
 \log D_\theta(0)
 +\mathbb{E}_{\Delta\sim q_\pi}\bigl[\log\bigl(1-D_\theta(\Delta)\bigr)\bigr].
 \label{eq:game}
\end{equation}
The first term asks the critic to accept perfect imitation; the second
asks it to reject the residuals of the current controller and, through
the replay in $q_\pi$, its recent history. Maximizing~\eqref{eq:game} is
the same as minimizing the balanced Bernoulli negative log-likelihood of
the labeling; writing $-\log\sigma(z)=\operatorname{softplus}(-z)$ and
$-\log\bigl(1-\sigma(z)\bigr)=\operatorname{softplus}(z)$ puts it in the
form we work with,
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DARE}}(\theta)
 =\tfrac12\operatorname{softplus}\bigl[-f_\theta(0)\bigr]
 +\tfrac12\mathbb{E}_{\Delta\sim q_\pi}
  \operatorname{softplus}\bigl[f_\theta(\Delta)\bigr].
 \label{eq:dare_loss}
\end{equation}
This is the differential objective of Section~\ref{sec:learned} without
its penalty. The labels state only whether a differential is ideal; the
relative importance of root, pose, and velocity residuals is left to
$f_\theta$, which estimates it from the residual distribution of the
current policy and re-estimates it as $q_\pi$ concentrates; this adaptive
aggregation is what replaces the manual scalarization~\eqref{eq:hand}.

The critic's verdict is the reward. $D_\theta(\Delta_t)$ is the
probability that the residual at step $t$ is ideal, so the policy should
be paid the more, the smaller $1-D_\theta(\Delta_t)$ is; the conversion
used by AMP and ADD implements exactly this, and in terms of the score it
is a softplus:
\begin{equation}
 r_t^{\mathrm{DARE}}
 =-\beta\log\bigl[1-D_\theta(\Delta_t)\bigr]
 =\beta\operatorname{softplus}\bigl[f_\theta(\Delta_t)\bigr],
 \label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
with a fixed amplitude $\beta$ shared by every skill. The reward is
non-negative, so it also pays the policy for staying alive, and it grows
without bound as the critic becomes more confident that the residual is
ideal; the same $f_\theta$ aggregates every tracking objective, so the
policy never consults a component distance, a success threshold, or a
component coefficient. What keeps~\eqref{eq:dare_loss} from producing the
delta function---and~\eqref{eq:reward} from collapsing to zero on every
state the policy visits---is not a penalty but the function class of
$f_\theta$; the next two subsections choose that class so that its
geometry treats the objectives fairly and its scale suits the reward.

\subsection{Equalized Critic: Geometry by Construction}
\label{sec:critic}
In the general differential of Section~\ref{sec:learned}, every objective
contributes one entry. For the $g$-th motion feature that entry is the
distance $l^g=\|\Delta_g\|_2$: a scalar that is $1$-Lipschitz in
$\Delta_g$ and vanishes exactly at the ideal point, and whose partial
derivative is the objective's weight. The tracking differential drops the
scalar and hands the critic the $d_g$ coordinates of $\Delta_g$ directly,
and a dense first layer treats them as follows. After per-coordinate
normalization every coordinate has unit typical magnitude, so
$\|\Delta_g\|_2$ grows like $\sqrt{d_g}$: an $84$-dimensional
body-rotation residual is about five times longer than a $3$-dimensional
root-position residual of the same per-coordinate size. Suppose the first
layer is a single dense map $W=[W_1\;\cdots\;W_G]$ whose gain is bounded,
$\sigma_{\max}(W)\le1$---the constraint we impose on every layer below to
obtain the geometric guarantee. Along any unit output direction $u$,
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{g=1}^{G}\bigl\|W_g^{\!\top}u\bigr\|_2^2
 =\bigl\|W^{\!\top}u\bigr\|_2^2\le 1,
 \label{eq:budget}
\end{equation}
so the $G$ blocks share one unit of gain in every output unit, and
gradient descent allocates that unit in proportion to the raw norms of
the blocks. The critic sees anonymous coordinates rather than objectives,
and the weights it learns follow the feature layout rather than the
skill. Root tracking is indeed where differential discriminators have
been reported to be least precise \cite{zhang2025add}.

DARE restores the per-objective structure without returning to a scalar.
Each factor $\Delta_g\in\mathbb{R}^{d_g}$ is mapped by its own affine map
and a $1$-Lipschitz activation $\rho$ to an equal-width latent, and the
latents are concatenated:
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \Phi_\oplus(\Delta)
 &=\bigoplus_{g=1}^{G}\psi_g(\Delta_g),\\
 \psi_g(\Delta_g)&=\rho(\bar{W}_g\Delta_g+b_g),
 \qquad
 \bar{W}_g=\frac{W_g}{\sigma_{\max}(W_g)},
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:directsum}
\end{equation}
where $W_g\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d_g}$ and every factor receives the same
latent width $m$. Each $\psi_g$ is $1$-Lipschitz in $\Delta_g$, exactly as
$l^g$ was; with $m=1$ and $W_g$ fixed to a unit row it reduces to a scalar
loss, and with $m>1$ and learned $W_g$ it is a vector-valued loss that
keeps the direction of the residual and adapts to the geometry of its
block. The direct sum keeps each $\psi_g$ in its own slot, so what follows
receives one equal-width entry per objective, as in the general form. A
shared trunk then scores the
concatenation, $g_\theta(\Delta)=\bar{T}(\Phi_\oplus(\Delta))$ with
$\bar{T}(h)=\bar{w}^{\!\top}\rho(\bar{V}h+c)+b$, whose linear maps are
spectrally normalized in the same way so that
$\operatorname{Lip}(\bar{T})\le1$. We write $g_\theta$ for this raw
critic output; the score $f_\theta$ that enters the loss~\eqref{eq:dare_loss}
and the reward~\eqref{eq:reward} is $g_\theta$ up to a single scalar
fixed in Section~\ref{sec:calib}, and equals $g_\theta$ until then. All
affine maps carry a bias, so
activation thresholds can sit where the residual distribution actually
lives; the anchor $g_\theta(0)$ thus depends on every layer and is trained
by the positive sample like any other point, while spectral normalization
keeps each map non-expansive regardless of the offsets.

Equalization gives every objective the same latent capacity and the same
gain bound before interaction. In contrast to~\eqref{eq:budget}, each
factor in~\eqref{eq:directsum} owns its output coordinates and attains
$\sigma_{\max}(\bar{W}_g)=1$ independently of the others. Because the
blocks act on disjoint outputs, their gains add in quadrature instead of
competing: for any two differentials,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \bigl\|\Phi_\oplus(\Delta)-\Phi_\oplus(\Delta')\bigr\|_2^2
 &=\sum_{g=1}^{G}
 \bigl\|\psi_g(\Delta_g)-\psi_g(\Delta'_g)\bigr\|_2^2\\
 &\le\|\Delta-\Delta'\|_2^2 ,
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:nonexp}
\end{equation}
since each block has Lipschitz constant at most one and the norm of a
concatenation is the root-sum of the block norms. The Jacobian of
$\Phi_\oplus$ is block diagonal across factors, so a perturbation in one
semantic subspace cannot be amplified through the coordinates of another,
and all $G$ factors attain unit gain simultaneously while the layer as a
whole stays non-expansive. The weight of objective $g$,
$\omega_g=\|\partial g_\theta/\partial\Delta_g\|_2$, is then bounded by
the trunk's sensitivity to its slot,
$\omega_g\le\|\partial g_\theta/\partial h_g\|_2$ with
$h_g=\psi_g(\Delta_g)$. The trunk still divides one unit of sensitivity
among the slots, but that division is now learned from the residual
distribution rather than dictated by coordinate count: the first layer no
longer lets a long block crowd out a short one before the trunk has seen
either. Equalization equalizes capacity and gain; it does not equalize
the norms $\|\Delta_g\|$, which are the sufficient statistics of each
block's evidence and are left intact.

The unique ideal point now gives the geometric guarantee. Because every
perfect pair occupies the origin, the quantity that governs reward
variation is the score change relative to the anchor, and
combining~\eqref{eq:nonexp} with $\operatorname{Lip}(\bar{T})\le1$ gives
\begin{equation}
 \bigl|g_\theta(0)-g_\theta(\Delta)\bigr|
 \le\|\Delta\|_2 .
 \label{eq:bound}
\end{equation}
If the realized motion changes only slightly relative to the reference,
the score changes by no more than the normalized residual; the biases
cancel in every difference, so they move the critic's activation
boundaries without loosening the bound. This is the geometric job of the
gradient penalty in Section~\ref{sec:learned}---nearby residuals must not
receive arbitrarily different scores---done by the parameterization: it
holds at initialization, at every training step, and for every motion,
with no coefficient.

\subsection{Anchor Calibration: Scale in Closed Form}
\label{sec:calib}
The bound~\eqref{eq:bound} says how steep the critic may be. It does not
say how steep the critic is, and the policy is sensitive to the
difference, because it receives the score through the fixed
transform~\eqref{eq:reward}. How much that reward responds to a change in
the score is its slope,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\partial r^{\mathrm{DARE}}}{\partial f_\theta}
 =\beta\,\sigma(f_\theta),
 \label{eq:slope}
\end{equation}
a sigmoid that rises from $0$ for very negative scores to $\beta$ for very
positive ones. Only over the transition of this sigmoid does a change in
the score change the reward. Far below the transition the reward is zero
however the residual changes; far above it the reward is linear in the
score and the transform has stopped shaping anything. The critic is
useful to the policy only where its scores fall on the transition.

Where they fall is decided by the loss, not by the bound. The output bias
of the critic shifts every score by the same amount, and the
loss~\eqref{eq:dare_loss} is stationary with respect to that shift when
its two terms have equal slope,
$\mathbb{E}_{q_\pi}\sigma\bigl(f_\theta(\Delta)\bigr)=\sigma\bigl(-f_\theta(0)\bigr)$.
The left-hand side is the mean probability of ideality the critic assigns
to policy residuals, and the score whose sigmoid equals that mean is the
natural representative of the negative class,
$\bar{f}_\theta=\sigma^{-1}\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{q_\pi}\sigma(f_\theta(\Delta))\bigr)$,
which we call the typical residual score. In these terms the stationarity
condition reads $\bar{f}_\theta=-f_\theta(0)$: writing
$M=f_\theta(0)-\bar{f}_\theta$ for the anchor gap,
\begin{equation}
 f_\theta(0)=+\tfrac{M}{2},
 \qquad
 \bar{f}_\theta=-\tfrac{M}{2}.
 \label{eq:symmetric}
\end{equation}
The anchor and the typical residual sit symmetrically about zero, and the
loss pushes $M$ as wide as the function class and the data permit.
($\bar{f}_\theta$ coincides with the arithmetic mean of the scores to
second order in their spread; the runs reported below computed $M$ with
the arithmetic mean, which differed from $\bar{f}_\theta$ by
\tbd\% at $t_f$.) The bound~\eqref{eq:bound} caps $M$ by the size of the
residuals themselves; the realized value is some fraction of that cap,
set by how much of the bound the trained network uses and by the residual
scale of the motion, neither of which is a design quantity. The two
failure modes of Section~\ref{sec:learned} are the two placements of $M$
relative to the transition of~\eqref{eq:slope}. If $M$ is too large, the typical residual
at $-M/2$ lies far below the transition: its reward and its reward slope
are both near zero, staying alive earns nothing, and improving a poor
residual earns nothing either. If $M$ is too small, the ratio of the
reward slope at the anchor to that at the typical residual,
$\sigma(M/2)/\sigma(-M/2)=e^{M/2}$, approaches one: the reward is close to
the constant $\beta\log2$ on every state, survival is rewarded, and the
differences that distinguish good tracking from bad are compressed. A
gradient penalty moves $M$ only indirectly, through the critic's slope on
sampled residuals, so the same $\lambda$ lands $M$ in different places
for different motions. DARE sets $M$ directly.

Both endpoints must therefore lie on the part of the transition where the
slope is neither negligible nor saturated, and the question is how wide
that part is. A sigmoid has no sharp edges; the conventional way to give
its transition a width---the same convention used for the rise time of
any sigmoidal response---is the span over which it climbs from $20\%$ to
$80\%$ of its maximum. For the slope~\eqref{eq:slope} this span is
\begin{equation}
 w=\sigma^{-1}(0.8)-\sigma^{-1}(0.2)=\ln4-(-\ln4)=\ln 16\approx 2.77 .
 \label{eq:width}
\end{equation}
Placing the anchor at the upper end of the transition and the typical
residual at the lower end means, by~\eqref{eq:symmetric}, $M=w$. The
choice has a direct reading in terms of the critic itself:
$D_\theta(0)=\sigma(\ln4)=0.8$ and $D_\theta(\bar\Delta)=0.2$, so the
calibrated critic assigns the anchor an $80\%$ probability of being ideal
and the typical residual $20\%$---a confident but not categorical
classifier. A larger $M$ drives it toward a hard classifier whose reward
vanishes on typical residuals, a smaller $M$ toward a coin flip whose
reward carries no information; keeping an adversarial critic in a band of
moderate confidence is standard practice \cite{karras2020ada}. The
convention is fixed a priori and never selected by task outcome, and it
passes an independent check: on the hardest skill in our benchmark
(Section~\ref{sec:opexp}), an uncalibrated critic that succeeded reached
a gap at $t_f$ within $2\%$ of $\ln16$, without either quantity having
been fitted to the other.

DARE achieves $M=w$ with one scalar applied to the raw critic $g_\theta$
of Section~\ref{sec:critic}:
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 f_\theta(\Delta)&=\kappa\,g_\theta(\Delta),
 \qquad
 \kappa=\frac{w}{M_f},\\
 M_f&=g_\theta(0)-\sigma^{-1}\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{q_\pi}\sigma(g_\theta(\Delta))\bigr)\Big|_{t=t_f}.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}
The gap $M_f$ is measured once, at the iteration $t_f$ at which the input
normalizer $\mathcal{N}$ is fixed, over the same mixture of current and
replayed residuals that forms the negative class of~\eqref{eq:dare_loss};
$\kappa$ is held constant thereafter, and before $t_f$ it equals one. The
moment is forced by the units: before $t_f$ the coordinates of $\Delta$
are still being rescaled and $M$ has no stable meaning. Both the
loss~\eqref{eq:dare_loss} and the reward~\eqref{eq:reward} use the
calibrated $f_\theta$, so the critic is trained and read out at one
scale.

Three properties follow. The anchor is untouched: $\kappa\cdot0=0$, and
$f_\theta(0)=\kappa g_\theta(0)$ is still trained by the positive sample
through every layer. The geometry is untouched: $\kappa$ rescales the
critic uniformly, so the ordering of residuals is preserved
and~\eqref{eq:bound} becomes
$|f_\theta(0)-f_\theta(\Delta)|\le\kappa\|\Delta\|_2$, a Lipschitz
constant derived from the data rather than assumed to be one. And the
rule contains no motion-specific number: $w$ is a constant of the reward
transform and $M_f$ a statistic of the training data, so, like the
normalizer $\mathcal{N}$, $\kappa$ takes a different value on every
motion because the same formula meets different residuals. Architecture,
optimizer, replay, and reward amplitude remain fixed choices inherited
from AMP and ADD; what DARE removes is the coefficient that had to be
chosen per motion.

\subsection{Training}
Training alternates rollout collection, differential construction, critic
updates, and PPO updates (Algorithm~\ref{alg:dare}). Critic minibatches
draw an equal mixture of current and replay differentials, with the ideal
point presented once per minibatch as the positive class; at $t_f$,
before the rewards of that iteration are computed, $M_f$ is measured with
the critic in evaluation mode and $\kappa$ is set by~\eqref{eq:kappa}. One
critic, one reward, one policy, and one calibration rule make up the whole
loop, and every quantity in it is shared across motions.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{DARE Training}
\label{alg:dare}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE reference motion $\mathcal{M}$, policy $\pi_\varphi$,
value $V_\omega$, raw critic $g_\theta$, replay buffer $\mathcal{B}$,
normalizer $\mathcal{N}$; $\kappa\leftarrow 1$
\FOR{iteration $t=1,2,\ldots$}
  \STATE Collect a synchronized rollout with $\pi_\varphi$ from random
  reference phases; construct $\Delta_t$ by~\eqref{eq:delta}; add to $\mathcal{B}$
  \IF{$\mathcal{N}$ was fixed this iteration}
    \STATE $\bar g\leftarrow\sigma^{-1}\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{B}\cup\text{rollout}}\,\sigma(g_\theta(\Delta))\bigr)$
    \STATE $M_f\leftarrow g_\theta(0)-\bar g$;\quad $\kappa\leftarrow\ln16/M_f$
  \ENDIF
  \STATE Compute $r_t^{\mathrm{DARE}}$ by~\eqref{eq:reward} with
  $f_\theta=\kappa g_\theta$
  \FOR{critic minibatch}
    \STATE Sample equal numbers of current and replay differentials;
    update $g_\theta$ by one step on~\eqref{eq:dare_loss} with
    $f_\theta=\kappa g_\theta$
  \ENDFOR
  \STATE Update $\pi_\varphi$ and $V_\omega$ with PPO on
  $r_t^{\mathrm{DARE}}$
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
All methods control the $28$-DoF, $15$-body simulated humanoid of
DeepMimic and ADD with PD joint targets at $30\,\mathrm{Hz}$ in Isaac Lab
($120\,\mathrm{Hz}$ physics) \cite{mittal2023isaaclab,makoviychuk2021isaacgym}.
Episodes start at a random reference phase and terminate on undesired
ground contact; DeepMimic, ADD, and DARE use pose termination with a unit
threshold, AMP follows its standard protocol without it. Each policy is
trained for $2000$ iterations of $8192$ environments $\times$ $32$ steps
($5.24\times10^8$ samples); the input normalizer is fixed after $10^{8}$
samples, at iteration $382$, where DARE calibrates. Actor and value
networks are two-layer MLPs ($1024$, $512$); PPO settings (clip $0.2$, GAE
$0.95$, $\gamma=0.99$, SGD with momentum $0.9$) are shared by all
learned-reward methods.

The six skills span distinct dynamical regimes: cyclic ground rotation
(Roll, $2.00\,\mathrm{s}$), long-horizon object interaction (Climb,
$9.97\,\mathrm{s}$), aerial inversion (Backflip, $1.75\,\mathrm{s}$),

$3.03\,\mathrm{s}$), and a ballistic kick (Spin Kick, $1.28\,\mathrm{s}$).

\section{Conclusion}
Manual tracking rewards turn the aggregation of heterogeneous imitation
objectives into per-motion engineering, and learned differential rewards,
having removed the weights, leave one coefficient that must still be
found per motion because it decides two things at once: how steep the
critic may be and how far the typical residual is pushed from the anchor.
DARE decides each separately, with no coefficient chosen per motion.
Every objective enters through its own spectrally normalized slot, so a
three-dimensional root residual and an eighty-four-dimensional rotation
residual stand on equal footing and the score change relative to the
unique zero differential is bounded by the residual; the critic is then
rescaled once, in closed form, so that the anchor and the typical
residual sit at the two ends of the reward slope's transition.

Extending DARE to multi-clip datasets and to other
morphologies requires no change to the rule, and the factorized critic
offers a direct place to attach task objectives as further semantic
blocks.
