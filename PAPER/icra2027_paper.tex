\begin{abstract}
Physics-based humanoid motion imitation must simultaneously satisfy several
heterogeneous objectives, including global root motion, articulated pose,
and velocities, keeping them consistent with a synchronized reference
trajectory. Handcrafted rewards combine these objectives with manually
designed weights and scales, whereas adversarial imitation can learn a
unified imitation signal from data through a discriminator, reducing the
reliance on explicit reward weights. For differential discriminators that
take the reference--policy differential as input, the sensitivity of the
score function and its numerical range still have to be controlled;
otherwise, changes in the residual distribution across motions can make the
same regularization setting produce different effective learning signals.
We propose DARE, a differential adversarial reward method that requires no
motion-specific coefficient and controls the geometry of the score
function and the score scale separately. DARE maps each semantic residual
block through its own map into a representation of a common latent width,
with an independent spectrally normalized gain bound imposed on each map,
thereby bounding the score change relative to the unique zero-error
anchor. DARE then performs a one-shot closed-form score calibration
according to the response window of the reward transform, with the global
scaling factor determined automatically from the residual distribution
observed during training. We evaluate DARE with a single configuration on
six humanoid skills with diverse dynamical characteristics and contact
patterns. DARE attains the lowest cross-motion macro-average error on all
four geometric tracking measures, the foot progress closest to the
reference, and the lowest lateral drift; the resulting controllers are
further deployed on
\todo{robot platform}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Physics-based humanoid motion imitation requires the controller to
reproduce a prescribed reference motion under dynamic constraints,
actuator limits, and complex contact conditions. Whether an imitation
succeeds is not a single geometric question. The root must translate and
turn as the reference does, the articulated body must maintain consistent
poses, joint and root velocities must stay synchronized with the reference
motion, and contacts must occur at the right moments. Because these
objectives have different physical units, numerical ranges, and dynamical
characteristics, and are coupled through the robot dynamics, motion
imitation is inherently a multi-objective control problem.

Traditional methods design a separate reward term for each type of
tracking error and combine them into a scalar reward through manually
chosen weights and scales. A representative form is
\begin{equation}
 r_t^{\mathrm{hand}}
 =\sum_{g=1}^{G} w_g
 \exp\!\Bigl[-\alpha_g\bigl\|\phi_g(\hat{s}_t)-\phi_g(s_t)\bigr\|_2^2\Bigr],
 \label{eq:hand}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_g$ extracts the $g$-th motion feature---joint rotations, root
position, velocities, or end-effector positions---and $s_t$ and
$\hat{s}_t$ denote the current simulated state and the synchronized
reference state. The weights $w_g$ decide the relative importance among
tracking objectives, and the scales $\alpha_g$ fix the numerical range of
each error in the reward. The difficulty is that typical error magnitudes,
dynamical difficulty, and the relative importance of the objectives change
with the motion, so a coefficient set that serves one skill need not serve
another; in practice, reward design is re-tuned whenever the motion
changes.

Adversarial imitation offers a way to reduce manual reward design.
Instead of declaring in advance how many meters of root-position error
are worth how many radians of rotation error, a discriminator learns a
unified imitation signal directly from data. AMP and related methods
learn rewards that describe motion style and plausibility by
discriminating whether short state transitions come from the reference
distribution \cite{peng2021amp}. For motion tracking that must strictly
follow a synchronized reference trajectory, ADD further feeds the
reference--policy state differential to the discriminator and asks
whether it is close to the ideal zero differential \cite{zhang2025add}.
The aggregation among tracking objectives can then be learned by the
discriminator, instead of specifying the per-term reward weights and
scales of~\eqref{eq:hand} by hand.

Differential adversarial rewards, however, still face an important issue.
Their ideal sample is always a single point, the zero differential.
Without a suitable constraint, the discriminator easily becomes too
sharp: it assigns a high score to the origin and compresses the many
nonzero residuals produced by the policy into a nearby low-score region.
Even if classification succeeds, the discriminator may not provide an
effective continuous learning signal for the policy. Existing methods
constrain the discriminator through regularization, but one regularizer
simultaneously affects two distinct properties. On the one hand, it
controls the discriminator's local sensitivity, that is, how fast the
score changes with the input residual. On the other hand, it also affects
the numerical range in which the final scores live, and this range in turn
determines whether the policy reward operates in an effective interval.
As the residual distribution changes across motions, the same
regularization setting can correspond to different sensitivities and
different score scales, producing motion-dependent effective
regularization requirements.

The core idea of DARE is to treat these two issues separately rather than
control them with one motion-dependent coefficient. Consider first the
critic's sensitivity to different tracking objectives. For any motion and
any phase, perfect imitation always corresponds to the same zero
differential $\Delta=0$, which we call the anchor. Meanwhile, the
differential in motion tracking naturally decomposes into semantic blocks
with clear physical meaning, such as root motion, body pose, and
velocities. Different tracking objectives occupy residual blocks of
different dimensions, which can couple representation size to critic
sensitivity. DARE first maps each semantic block through its own
spectrally normalized map to a common-width latent representation before
shared aggregation; the shared critic then learns the relations among
objectives from the residuals that actually occur during training. The
concrete construction and the corresponding Lipschitz properties are
given in Section~\ref{sec:critic}.

Constraining the critic's sensitivity alone does not resolve the score
scale. A Lipschitz bound controls how rapidly the score may change, but
not the numerical range actually used by the trained critic. DARE
therefore measures the anchor-to-typical-residual score gap once after
normalization stabilizes and uses a closed-form global rescaling tied to
the response range of the reward transform. All motions use exactly the
same calibration rule; the only quantity that changes across motions is
the residual statistic measured automatically from the training data.
The critic's geometry and score scale thus no longer depend on the same
hand-tuned parameter.

The main contributions are threefold. First, we identify that the
regularizer in existing differential adversarial rewards simultaneously
affects the critic's local geometry and its score scale, and that this
coupling can make different motions require different effective
regularization settings. Second, we propose DARE, which handles the
semantic residual blocks separately through a factorized spectrally
normalized critic and sets the score scale automatically by a one-shot
anchor calibration, removing the need to re-specify reward or
regularization coefficients for each motion. Third, we evaluate one DARE
configuration on six skills with different motion durations, contact
patterns, and dynamical characteristics, and deploy the resulting
controllers on
\todo{robot platform}.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Physics-based motion tracking and learned imitation objectives.}
DeepMimic combines pose, velocity, root, and end-effector tracking terms
for physics-based skills \cite{peng2018deepmimic}; subsequent work
improves scalability and motion coverage through architectures,
representations, and data \cite{luo2023phc,tessler2023maskedmimic}.
Adversarial imitation provides a learned alternative to a manually
written reward: GAIL matches expert and policy occupancies
\cite{ho2016gail}, while AMP learns a motion prior by discriminating
short state transitions \cite{peng2021amp}. ADD addresses
reference-conditioned tracking by presenting the reference--policy
differential to an adversarial discriminator and learning a nonlinear
aggregation of tracking objectives without component-wise reward weights
\cite{zhang2025add}.

\textbf{Multi-objective learning and objective aggregation.} Competing
objectives are commonly combined by weighted scalarization, Pareto
optimization, or adaptive loss weighting
\cite{sener2018multitask,kendall2018multi}. These approaches differ in
whether preferences are specified explicitly or adapted from objective
statistics. Motion tracking adds a representation issue: one physical
objective can occupy a feature block with many more coordinates than
another, even when both are conceptually single objectives.

\textbf{Regularization of adversarial critics.} Adversarial critics are
regularized with gradient penalties or spectral normalization to control
sensitivity and improve optimization
\cite{gulrajani2017improved,miyato2018spectral}. Gradient penalties
constrain sampled input derivatives, whereas spectral normalization
bounds the gain of individual network maps by construction. For
non-Wasserstein adversarial losses, the numerical score range also
determines the operating regime of the loss and its reward transform
\cite{karras2020ada}. Geometry and score calibration are therefore
related but distinct considerations when a critic supplies the learning
signal.

\section{Background}
\subsection{Reference-Conditioned Motion Tracking}
In reference-conditioned humanoid motion imitation, the controller must
keep the executed motion continuously aligned with a time-synchronized
reference trajectory. Let $s_t$ and $\hat{s}_t$ denote the current state
and the reference state at time $t$. Tracking quality is jointly measured
by several physical quantities, including root position and orientation,
articulated body pose, and motion velocities. We organize these
quantities into $G$ semantic feature groups: $\phi_g(s_t)$ extracts the
feature vector of group $g$ from the current state, and
$\phi_g(\hat{s}_t)$ its counterpart from the reference state. Different
groups may have different dimensions and physical units---positions are
measured in meters, while rotations and velocities have their own
representations.

\subsection{Handcrafted Objective Scalarization}
Traditional methods first compress each tracking error into a scalar.
For group $g$, the scalar tracking error at time $t$ is the Euclidean
distance
$e_{g,t}=\|\phi_g(\hat{s}_t)-\phi_g(s_t)\|_2$.
The errors $e_{g,t}$ are then transformed and combined into a single
policy reward. DeepMimic is a representative instance of this class: its
tracking terms use weights and error scales specified before training and
held fixed throughout \cite{peng2018deepmimic}. This is a fixed
scalarization of the tracking objectives: the trade-off among errors is
fixed through constant coefficients chosen before training starts.
Because typical error magnitudes and dynamical characteristics differ
across motions, one coefficient set need not serve all motions. Learned
scalarization instead leaves the trade-off unspecified in advance and
learns the aggregation among objectives from the error distribution that
actually occurs during training.

\subsection{Differential Adversarial Objectives}
\label{sec:learned}
Differential adversarial learning provides such a learned aggregation
\cite{zhang2025add}. Consider $n$ non-negative objective losses
$l^1,\ldots,l^n$ collected into a differential vector
$\Delta=(l^1,\ldots,l^n)$, each entry recording the gap between the
current solution and the ideal on one objective; when all objectives are
simultaneously ideal, $\Delta=0$. A discriminator $D(\Delta)$ is trained
to separate the ideal zero differential from the nonzero differentials
produced by the current policy. Since the discriminator receives the
differentials of all objectives at once, it can learn a nonlinear
objective aggregation function without fixed per-objective weights. Its
local sensitivity to objective $i$ is described by
$\partial D/\partial l^i$: the extent to which the discriminator output
responds when objective $i$ changes slightly near the current
differential. Unlike fixed scalarization weights, this local sensitivity
can vary with the position in the residual space and during training.
In synchronized motion tracking, the differential is no longer
restricted to one scalar loss per objective but can be formed directly
from the feature residual between the current and reference states.
Perfect tracking again corresponds to the unique zero differential, while
nonzero differentials retain the error direction and the relations among
features. The policy reward is generated from the discriminator's score
on the current differential, for example
$r_t=-\log(1-D(\Delta_t))$: the closer the current residual is judged to
the ideal state, the higher the reward.

A peculiarity of this form is that the ideal class always concentrates on
the single point $\Delta=0$. If the discriminator is overly sensitive to
input changes, it can easily separate the origin from nonzero residuals,
yet fail to form a smooth and useful score among policy residuals of
different quality. Differential adversarial methods therefore constrain
the local sensitivity of the score function, for example with a sampled
gradient penalty
$\lambda\,\mathbb{E}_{\Delta\sim q_\pi}\|\nabla_\Delta D(\Delta)\|_2^2$,
where $q_\pi$ denotes the residual distribution produced by the current
policy \cite{zhang2025add}. Constraining the local sensitivity, however,
does not determine the score range the trained discriminator uses: since
the policy reward further passes through a nonlinear transform, both
properties affect the final learning signal, but they are not the same
question.

\section{DARE: Differential Adversarial Reward Equalization}
\label{sec:dare}
\subsection{Learned Differential Reward}
\label{sec:reward}
Motion tracking involves multiple objectives with different physical
meanings. For each objective, the scalar distance $e_{g,t}$ summarizes
the error magnitude and is well suited to a traditional weighted reward,
but it retains only that magnitude. The raw feature differential also
contains the error direction and the joint variation across coordinates.
DARE therefore takes the feature differentials directly as the input of
the learned reward. For semantic group $g$, the raw residual is
$\phi_g(\hat{s}_t)-\phi_g(s_t)$. Since positions, rotations, and
velocities have different units and typical ranges, we normalize every
coordinate first. Let the normalizer of group $g$ be
$\mathcal{N}_g=\operatorname{diag}(\nu_g)^{-1}$, where $\nu_g$ records
the mean absolute magnitude of each coordinate's residual, estimated from
residual samples in early training and fixed after the first $10^{8}$
samples, as is standard for adversarial motion rewards. The normalization
only rescales coordinates and introduces no offset, so the zero
differential of perfect tracking always stays at the origin; rotations use
a tangent--normal representation so that their residuals can be handled
uniformly in Euclidean coordinates.

After normalization, the group residual, the full differential, and the
ideal point are
\begin{equation}
 \Delta_{g,t}=\mathcal{N}_g\bigl(\phi_g(\hat{s}_t)-\phi_g(s_t)\bigr),
 \qquad
 \Delta_t=\bigoplus_{g=1}^{G}\Delta_{g,t},
 \qquad
 \Delta^{+}=0 .
 \label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}
The vector $\Delta_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$, $d=\sum_g d_g$, retains the
direction of each coordinate error and the structural relations formed
when several coordinates vary together. Whatever the reference motion and
whatever the current phase, perfectly consistent states map to the same
zero differential, while the nonzero differentials produced by the policy
record how the current motion deviates from the reference. During
training, recently visited differentials are kept in a replay buffer and
combined with the current ones into the policy residual distribution
$q_\pi$, which keeps evolving with the policy.

DARE learns a scalar score function on this differential space. Let
$f_\theta(\Delta)$ denote the raw scalar score of the critic, converted
by the logistic sigmoid into
$D_\theta(\Delta)=\sigma\bigl(f_\theta(\Delta)\bigr)$, which expresses
the degree to which the critic considers the differential ideal. The zero
differential represents perfect tracking, and the residuals produced by
the policy are drawn from $q_\pi$; the critic is therefore trained by the
following adversarial objective to distinguish the two:
\begin{equation}
 \max_{\theta}\;
 \log D_\theta(0)
 +\mathbb{E}_{\Delta\sim q_\pi}\bigl[\log\bigl(1-D_\theta(\Delta)\bigr)\bigr].
 \label{eq:game}
\end{equation}
The first term raises the ideality score of the zero differential, and
the second lowers the ideality score of current policy residuals. As the
policy improves, the residual distribution gradually moves toward the
origin, and the critic updates its score function accordingly. A function
$f_\theta$ that co-evolves with training thereby aggregates multiple
tracking objectives into a single scalar signal.

For optimization, we write the above binary classification objective as
a balanced Bernoulli negative log-likelihood. Using
$-\log\sigma(z)=\operatorname{softplus}(-z)$ and
$-\log\bigl(1-\sigma(z)\bigr)=\operatorname{softplus}(z)$ gives the
critic loss
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DARE}}(\theta)
 =\tfrac12\operatorname{softplus}\bigl[-f_\theta(0)\bigr]
 +\tfrac12\mathbb{E}_{\Delta\sim q_\pi}
  \operatorname{softplus}\bigl[f_\theta(\Delta)\bigr].
 \label{eq:dare_loss}
\end{equation}
This objective provides the critic with only two kinds of supervision,
ideal differentials and policy residuals; how root motion, body pose, and
velocities jointly determine the final score is learned by $f_\theta$
from the residual distribution observed during training.

The policy then constructs its reward directly from the critic score.
DARE defines
\begin{equation}
 r_t^{\mathrm{DARE}}
 =-\beta\log\bigl[1-D_\theta(\Delta_t)\bigr]
 =\beta\operatorname{softplus}\bigl[f_\theta(\Delta_t)\bigr],
 \label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ controls the overall reward amplitude and is held fixed
across all motions. The closer the critic judges the current differential
to the ideal state, the higher $f_\theta(\Delta_t)$ and the larger the
policy reward. The policy can thus improve root motion, body pose, and
velocities simultaneously through one unified scalar reward.

This formulation recasts multi-objective tracking as learning a scalar
score function in the differential space, and its effect depends on two
properties of the critic itself. First, the semantic feature groups in
the differential have different dimensions, and the critic's sensitivity
to each group's residual variation requires a clear and consistent
structural constraint. Second, the scores produced by the critic are
converted into the policy reward through the softplus transform, so the
numerical range of the scores directly determines how strongly the reward
responds to changes in tracking quality. DARE states these two properties
as critic geometry and score scale, and designs for them independently.

\subsection{Equalized Critic: Geometry by Construction}
\label{sec:critic}
The full differential $\Delta$ consists of $G$ semantic feature blocks,
the $g$-th block denoted $\Delta_g\in\mathbb{R}^{d_g}$, where $d_g$ is
the feature dimension of that objective. Different objectives in motion
tracking use different physical representations, so their coordinate
counts usually differ. Per-coordinate normalization makes the typical
numerical scales of coordinates comparable, but the overall norm of a
feature block is still influenced by its dimension. When coordinates have
comparable typical magnitudes, the block norm $\|\Delta_g\|_2$ generally
grows with $\sqrt{d_g}$. Consequently, if all semantic blocks were
concatenated and fed directly into one shared dense layer, the raw
representation dimension would participate in deciding how strongly the
network responds to each objective's residual variation.

Let this shared linear map be $W=[W_1\;\cdots\;W_G]$, where $W_g$ acts on
the $g$-th semantic block. The largest singular value $\sigma_{\max}(W)$
of a linear map expresses the maximum amplification it can apply to an
input change, and imposing
$\sigma_{\max}(W)\le1$
means that distances between any two points are at most preserved---a
$1$-Lipschitz property. For any unit output direction $u$, the shared map
satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{g=1}^{G}\bigl\|W_g^{\!\top}u\bigr\|_2^2
 =\bigl\|W^{\!\top}u\bigr\|_2^2\le 1,
 \label{eq:budget}
\end{equation}
which shows that the semantic blocks are jointly subject to one overall
gain constraint in each output direction. A shared layer can bound the
maximum amplification of the whole map, but the gains of the blocks are
allocated by the same matrix, so its response is coupled to the input
representation itself. For semantic blocks of different dimensions, this
coupling lets the representation dimension influence how sensitive the
critic is to each objective's residual variation.

DARE instead builds an independent constrained representation for each
semantic block before the blocks interact. The $g$-th block is mapped
into the latent space by
\begin{align}
 \psi_g(\Delta_g)&=\rho(\bar{W}_g\Delta_g+b_g),
 \qquad
 \bar{W}_g=\frac{W_g}{\sigma_{\max}(W_g)},
\label{eq:directsum}
\end{align}
where $\rho$ is a $1$-Lipschitz activation. Spectral normalization
rescales the largest singular value of each linear map to one, so each
$\psi_g$ carries its own independent $1$-Lipschitz gain bound:
$\|\psi_g(\Delta_g)-\psi_g(\Delta'_g)\|_2\le\|\Delta_g-\Delta'_g\|_2$.
All semantic blocks are mapped to the same latent width $m$
($W_g\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d_g}$, $\psi_g(\Delta_g)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}$),
giving each objective the same number of representation channels before
shared aggregation.

The latent representations of the blocks are then combined by a direct
sum,
$\Phi_\oplus(\Delta)=\bigoplus_{g=1}^{G}\psi_g(\Delta_g)$,
where $\bigoplus$ places each $m$-dimensional latent vector in mutually
disjoint coordinates; with $G$ semantic blocks the total dimension of
$\Phi_\oplus(\Delta)$ is $Gm$. Within-block features are thus processed
first by their own maps, and cross-objective information interaction
happens uniformly in the subsequent shared trunk. The shared trunk
aggregates these latent representations into a scalar score,
$g_\theta(\Delta)=\bar{T}\bigl(\Phi_\oplus(\Delta)\bigr)$ with
$\bar{T}(h)=\bar{w}^{\!\top}\rho(\bar{V}h+c)+b$, whose linear maps are
spectrally normalized in the same way so that
$\operatorname{Lip}(\bar{T})\le1$. Here $g_\theta$ denotes the raw critic
output before calibration; the score $f_\theta$ used in the loss and the
policy reward is identical to $g_\theta$ before the scale calibration and
afterwards differs by one fixed global scaling factor. All affine layers
keep their bias terms; biases shift activation boundaries but cancel in
score differences and therefore do not alter the Lipschitz bound.

The overall property of this block structure follows directly from a
distance relation. Because the latent representations of the semantic
blocks occupy non-overlapping coordinates, for any two differentials
$\Delta$ and $\Delta'$,
\begin{align}
 \bigl\|\Phi_\oplus(\Delta)-\Phi_\oplus(\Delta')\bigr\|_2^2
 &=\sum_{g=1}^{G}
 \bigl\|\psi_g(\Delta_g)-\psi_g(\Delta'_g)\bigr\|_2^2 \notag\\
 &\le\sum_{g=1}^{G}
 \bigl\|\Delta_g-\Delta'_g\bigr\|_2^2
 =\|\Delta-\Delta'\|_2^2 ,
\label{eq:nonexp}
\end{align}
so the entire map from the raw differential to the concatenated latent
representation remains $1$-Lipschitz. Each semantic block first passes
through its own independent constrained map, and the shared trunk then
learns the relations among objectives; how sensitive the critic should
actually be to each objective is learned by the shared trunk from the
residual distribution observed during training.

Combining the non-expansive latent maps with the Lipschitz bound of the
shared trunk yields, for the raw critic,
$|g_\theta(\Delta)-g_\theta(\Delta')|\le\|\Delta-\Delta'\|_2$. In motion
tracking, perfect imitation always corresponds to the zero differential
$\Delta=0$, the anchor; taking $\Delta'=0$ gives
\begin{equation}
 \bigl|g_\theta(0)-g_\theta(\Delta)\bigr|
 \le\|\Delta\|_2 .
 \label{eq:bound}
\end{equation}
This bound---the critic's geometry, i.e.\ how fast the score may change
with the input residual---is thus a direct consequence of the network
structure, independent of the motion.

\subsection{Anchor Calibration: Scale in Closed Form}
\label{sec:calib}
The bound~\eqref{eq:bound} limits how fast the critic score may change,
but not the numerical range the trained critic actually uses; that range
is decided jointly by the optimization process and the residual
distribution. It directly affects the reward received by the policy.
DARE converts the critic score into the policy reward through
$r^{\mathrm{DARE}}=\beta\operatorname{softplus}(f_\theta)$, so the rate
of change of the reward with respect to the score is
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\partial r^{\mathrm{DARE}}}{\partial f_\theta}
 =\beta\,\sigma(f_\theta).
 \label{eq:slope}
\end{equation}
When $f_\theta$ is very negative, score changes bring only tiny reward
differences; around zero the reward responds markedly to score changes;
for large $f_\theta$ the slope approaches $\beta$ and softplus becomes
nearly linear. The critic therefore needs not only a suitable sensitivity
to residuals but also scores that fall inside the range where the reward
transform is sufficiently discriminative.

This scale can be read from the adversarial loss itself. Let the anchor
score be $f_\theta(0)$. The residuals produced by the policy follow
$q_\pi$, and the critic's mean ideality probability over these residuals
is $\mathbb{E}_{\Delta\sim q_\pi}\sigma\bigl(f_\theta(\Delta)\bigr)$.
Mapping this mean probability back to the logit space defines the
representative score of the typical policy residual,
$\bar{f}_\theta=\sigma^{-1}\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{\Delta\sim q_\pi}
\sigma(f_\theta(\Delta))\bigr)$.
The balanced Bernoulli adversarial loss is stationary with respect to a
global output shift when
$\mathbb{E}_{q_\pi}\sigma\bigl(f_\theta(\Delta)\bigr)
=\sigma\bigl(-f_\theta(0)\bigr)$,
hence $\bar{f}_\theta=-f_\theta(0)$. Writing
$M=f_\theta(0)-\bar{f}_\theta$ for the score gap between the anchor and
the typical residual then gives
\begin{equation}
 f_\theta(0)=+\tfrac{M}{2},
 \qquad
 \bar{f}_\theta=-\tfrac{M}{2}.
 \label{eq:symmetric}
\end{equation}
At the reference placement induced by the balanced loss, the anchor score
and the typical policy residual score are symmetric about zero, and $M$
measures their separation in logit space.

This gap directly determines the operating regime of the reward signal.
When $M$ is too large, typical residuals fall into the low-slope region
of the reward transform, so improvements in poor tracking produce little
additional reward. When $M$ is too small, anchor and residual scores
cluster around zero and the reward differences among tracking qualities
are compressed. A useful score scale should therefore place their
separation across the main response region of the sigmoid.

A sigmoid has no strict transition boundary, so the operating region
needs an operational width. A common choice is the $20$--$80\%$ span of
the sigmoid, for which the corresponding logit width is
\begin{equation}
 w=\sigma^{-1}(0.8)-\sigma^{-1}(0.2)=\ln4-(-\ln4)=\ln 16\approx 2.77 .
 \label{eq:width}
\end{equation}
DARE adopts $w=\ln16$ as the critic's target operating gap. Under the
symmetric reference placement, this width corresponds to $+w/2$ and
$-w/2$, whose sigmoid values are $0.8$ and $0.2$, respectively---a
confident but not categorical classifier, in line with the common
practice of keeping an adversarial critic in a band of moderate
confidence \cite{karras2020ada}. This operating region is fixed by the
reward transform before training and is identical across all motions.

The critic obtained in actual training need not naturally use this
scale. DARE therefore measures the score gap $M_f$ of the current critic
once, after the input normalization stabilizes, and rescales the whole
logit uniformly by
\begin{align}
 f_\theta(\Delta)&=\kappa\,g_\theta(\Delta),
 \qquad
 \kappa=\frac{w}{M_f}, \notag\\
 M_f&=g_\theta(0)-\sigma^{-1}\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{q_\pi}\sigma(g_\theta(\Delta))\bigr)\Big|_{t=t_f},
\label{eq:kappa}
\end{align}
where $g_\theta$ is the raw critic output before calibration and
$f_\theta$ the calibrated score that enters the adversarial loss and the
policy reward. When the measured gap $M_f$ exceeds the target width $w$,
$\kappa=w/M_f<1$ and all critic scores are compressed toward zero,
bringing an overly wide score gap back into the main response range of
the reward transform; conversely, $M_f<w$ gives $\kappa>1$ and the
critic scores expand away from zero, enlarging the reward differences
among tracking qualities. Because the typical residual score used in
practice is defined through $\sigma^{-1}(\mathbb{E}\sigma(\cdot))$, the
goal of the calibration is to move the overall logit scale toward the
operating range set by $w$; it does not require every post-calibration
statistic to land exactly on a prescribed endpoint.

The gap $M_f$ is measured once at the iteration $t_f$ at which the input
normalizer $\mathcal{N}$ is fixed. The computation uses the mixture of
current-rollout and replayed residuals, consistent with the non-ideal
sample distribution used in critic training. Before $t_f$, $\kappa=1$:
only after the normalizer is fixed do the units and scales of the
differential coordinates stop changing, so a measured score gap first
acquires a stable numerical meaning then. After the calibration,
$\kappa$ is held fixed, and both the critic loss and the policy reward
use $f_\theta=\kappa g_\theta$; the critic continues to train at one
scale, and the policy reads its reward at the same scale.

The uniform rescaling preserves the critic's original score ordering and
multiplies the raw score function by the same constant. The
pre-calibration geometric bound
$|g_\theta(0)-g_\theta(\Delta)|\le\|\Delta\|_2$
accordingly becomes
$|f_\theta(0)-f_\theta(\Delta)|\le\kappa\|\Delta\|_2$,
a Lipschitz constant derived from the data rather than assumed to be
one. The target width $w=\ln16$ is fixed by the reward transform, and
$M_f$ is measured automatically from the training data, so all motions
share the same calibration rule while the resulting $\kappa$ varies
automatically with each motion's residual distribution.

\subsection{Training}
Training alternates rollout collection, differential construction, critic
updates, and PPO updates (Algorithm~\ref{alg:dare}). Critic minibatches
draw an equal mixture of current and replay differentials, with the ideal
point presented once per minibatch as the positive class; at $t_f$,
before the rewards of that iteration are computed, $M_f$ is measured with
the critic in evaluation mode and $\kappa$ is set by~\eqref{eq:kappa}. One
critic, one reward, one policy, and one calibration rule make up the whole
loop.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{DARE Training}
\label{alg:dare}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE reference motion $\mathcal{M}$, policy $\pi_\varphi$,
value $V_\omega$, raw critic $g_\theta$, replay buffer $\mathcal{B}$,
normalizer $\mathcal{N}$; $\kappa\leftarrow 1$
\FOR{iteration $t=1,2,\ldots$}
  \STATE Collect a synchronized rollout with $\pi_\varphi$ from random
  reference phases; construct $\Delta_t$ by~\eqref{eq:delta}; add to $\mathcal{B}$
  \IF{$\mathcal{N}$ was fixed this iteration}
    \STATE $\bar g\leftarrow\sigma^{-1}\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{B}\cup\text{rollout}}\,\sigma(g_\theta(\Delta))\bigr)$
    \STATE $M_f\leftarrow g_\theta(0)-\bar g$;\quad $\kappa\leftarrow\ln16/M_f$
  \ENDIF
  \STATE Compute $r_t^{\mathrm{DARE}}$ by~\eqref{eq:reward} with
  $f_\theta=\kappa g_\theta$
  \FOR{critic minibatch}
    \STATE Sample equal numbers of current and replay differentials;
    update $g_\theta$ by one step on~\eqref{eq:dare_loss} with
    $f_\theta=\kappa g_\theta$
  \ENDFOR
  \STATE Update $\pi_\varphi$ and $V_\omega$ with PPO on
  $r_t^{\mathrm{DARE}}$
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
All methods are evaluated using the same $28$-DoF, $15$-body humanoid
model adopted in prior physics-based motion-imitation studies, simulated in
Isaac Lab at $120\,\mathrm{Hz}$ with actions applied at $30\,\mathrm{Hz}$
through PD joint-position control
\cite{mittal2023isaaclab,makoviychuk2021isaacgym}. All four methods share
the same environment, training budget, motion clips, random-phase
initialization, and evaluation protocol. Each policy is trained for
$2000$ iterations of $8192$ environments $\times$ $32$ steps
($5.24\times10^8$ samples) with five independent seeds, and is evaluated
on $4096$ episodes per seed from randomly sampled reference phases. Actor
and value networks are two-layer MLPs ($1024$, $512$), and the PPO
settings (clip $0.2$, GAE $0.95$, $\gamma=0.99$, SGD with momentum $0.9$)
are fixed across methods. DARE additionally uses the normalizer and
one-shot logit calibration specified in Table~\ref{tab:config}.

The benchmark contains six skills with substantially different motion
durations, contact patterns, and dynamical characteristics: Roll, Climb,
Backflip, Crawl, Get-Up, and Spin Kick. Training termination follows each
method's environment configuration (pose termination is enabled for
DeepMimic, ADD, and DARE and disabled for AMP). The evaluation harness
accumulates each metric over the executed reference-aligned steps and uses
the same aggregation rule for every method; terminated trajectories are not
padded with synthetic states. Final policies reach essentially the full
prescribed evaluation horizon, so early termination has negligible effect
on the reported averages.

We use six complementary measures. The four geometric measures quantify
frame-wise imitation fidelity, whereas foot progress and lateral drift capture
trajectory-level behavior that is not summarized by instantaneous pose errors.
Four describe geometric tracking at two spatial levels: root position and
root rotation measure global translation and orientation, while body position
and body rotation measure articulated-body tracking. For an evaluation
trajectory with $B$ bodies, where $r,p_b$ and $q,q_b$ denote simulated root,
body positions and orientations and hats denote reference values, these
quantities are
\begingroup
\setlength{\abovedisplayskip}{0pt plus 1pt minus 1pt}
\setlength{\belowdisplayskip}{0pt plus 1pt minus 1pt}
\begin{align}
 E_{\mathrm{root\text{-}pos}}&=\lVert\hat r-r\rVert_2,
 \quad
 E_{\mathrm{root\text{-}rot}}=\angle(q,\hat q), \notag\\
 E_{\mathrm{body\text{-}pos}}&=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}
   \left\lVert(\hat p_b-\hat r)-(p_b-r)\right\rVert_2, \notag\\
 E_{\mathrm{body\text{-}rot}}&=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\angle(q_b,\hat q_b).
\label{eq:geometric_metrics}
\end{align}
\endgroup
where $\angle(\cdot,\cdot)$ is the shortest angle between normalized
quaternions. Geometric quantities are evaluated at each reference-aligned
timestep and then averaged over executed timesteps and episodes; the two
behavior measures are computed per trajectory and then averaged over episodes.
The remaining two measures describe motion-level behavior.
Let $\Delta r$ and $\Delta\hat r$ be the simulated and reference root
displacements over the evaluated trajectory, and let
$\Delta p_j,\Delta\hat p_j$ be the corresponding displacements of foot $j$.
The reference-projected progress and transverse drift are
\begin{align}
 \rho_{\mathrm{foot}}&=\min_j
 \frac{\Delta p_j^{\top}\Delta\hat p_j}
      {\lVert\Delta\hat p_j\rVert_2^2}, \notag\\
 \rho_r&=\frac{\Delta r^{\top}\Delta\hat r}
                 {\lVert\Delta\hat r\rVert_2^2}, \notag\\
 E_{\mathrm{lat}}&=
 \frac{\left\lVert\Delta r-
 \rho_r\Delta\hat r\right\rVert_2}{\lVert\Delta\hat r\rVert_2}.
\label{eq:behavior_metrics}
\end{align}
Foot progress is ideal at one, while lateral drift is minimized at zero.
All six quantities are averaged over the evaluation trajectories; lower is
better except for foot progress, whose target value is one.

\subsection{Cross-Motion Tracking}
Table~\ref{tab:main} asks whether one configuration can serve skills whose
contacts, horizons, and velocity profiles differ, and
Fig.~\ref{fig:snapshots} shows the resulting controllers executing the six
skills in Isaac Lab. With one configuration shared across all six skills,
DARE achieves the best macro-average on all six measures. It yields the
lowest root- and body-level position and rotation errors, the
foot-progress ratio closest to one, and the lowest lateral drift.
Relative to the strongest baseline for each measure, root-position error,
root-rotation error, and lateral drift are reduced by approximately
$25\%$, $21\%$, and $38\%$.

Figure~\ref{fig:learning} compares the five-seed mean body-position and

complementing the final-checkpoint comparison in Table~\ref{tab:main}.

\section{Conclusion}
Manual tracking rewards turn the aggregation of heterogeneous imitation
objectives into per-motion engineering. Learned differential rewards
remove the component-wise weights, but regularization can still couple the
critic's geometric sensitivity to the score range seen by the policy.
DARE treats these as separate design quantities: the factorized critic
specifies the geometry, and one closed-form calibration sets the score
scale without a motion-specific coefficient. Every objective enters
through its own spectrally normalized slot, so feature blocks of unequal
dimension enter through equal-width, independently normalized
representations, and the score change relative to the unique zero
differential is bounded by the residual; the critic is then rescaled once,
in closed form, to set its operating scale relative to the transition
width of the reward transform. Across the six skills, DARE attains the
lowest macro-average for all four geometric errors, the foot-progress
ratio closest to one, and the smallest lateral drift in
Table~\ref{tab:main}. The formulation also provides a direct path toward
multi-clip training and other morphologies, where additional objectives
can be introduced as semantic blocks.
