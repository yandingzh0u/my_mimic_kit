\title{\LARGE \bf MIRA: Monotonic Imitation Rewards via Adversarial Learning}
\author{Anonymous Authors}

\begin{abstract}
Humanoid motion imitation requires jointly considering multiple tracking
objectives, including position, orientation, and velocity. Conventional
reward functions rely on manually designed weights and scales, often
requiring repeated tuning for different motions. This paper presents MIRA,
a monotonic imitation reward method based on adversarial learning. MIRA
represents deviations between the current state and a synchronized reference
state as nonnegative physical errors. It constructs a monotone discriminator
using nonnegative weights and learnable diagonal normalization to adaptively
learn trade-offs among different errors while ensuring that reducing errors
cannot decrease the reward. Experimental results show that MIRA achieves
accurate and stable motion tracking across different motions with a unified
reward configuration.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Physics-based motion imitation requires a humanoid to track position,
orientation, and velocity while obeying dynamics and contact constraints.
These objectives have different units and can compete. Conventional
rewards combine them using manually selected weights and scales that may
need retuning with a change of motion.

Adversarial imitation offers a way to reduce manual reward design.
Instead of declaring in advance how many meters of root-position error
are worth how many radians of rotation error, a discriminator learns a
unified imitation signal directly from data. AMP and related methods
learn rewards that describe motion style and plausibility by
discriminating whether short state transitions come from the reference
distribution \cite{peng2021amp}. For motion tracking that must strictly
follow a synchronized reference trajectory, ADD further feeds the
reference--policy state differential to the discriminator and asks
whether it is close to the ideal zero differential \cite{zhang2025add}.
The aggregation among tracking objectives can then be learned by the
discriminator instead of specifying component-wise weights and scales by
hand.

Differential classification alone neither orders nonzero errors by
physical tracking quality nor bounds score sensitivity. A smaller
difference in a rotation representation, for example, can correspond to
a larger angular error. MIRA addresses both problems by learning on
componentwise physical errors with normalized group scales. Its
nonnegative weights and monotone activations preserve the ordering of
physical errors, while learnable diagonal normalization bounds the
sensitivity to those errors without a gradient penalty
\cite{runje2023constrained,araujo2023unified}. We evaluate this reward on
six humanoid motions and isolate the effects of the weight signs,
group dimension scaling, and diagonal normalization.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Physics-based motion tracking and learned imitation objectives.}
DeepMimic combines pose, velocity, root, and end-effector tracking terms
for physics-based skills \cite{peng2018deepmimic}; subsequent work
improves scalability and motion coverage through architectures,
representations, and data \cite{luo2023phc,tessler2023maskedmimic}.
Adversarial imitation provides a learned alternative to a manually
written reward: GAIL matches expert and policy occupancies
\cite{ho2016gail}, while AMP learns a motion prior by discriminating
short state transitions \cite{peng2021amp}. ADD addresses
reference-conditioned tracking by presenting the reference--policy
differential to an adversarial discriminator and learning a nonlinear
aggregation of tracking objectives without component-wise reward weights
\cite{zhang2025add}.

\textbf{Multi-objective learning and objective aggregation.} Competing
objectives are commonly combined by weighted scalarization, Pareto
optimization, or adaptive loss weighting
\cite{sener2018multitask,kendall2018multi}. These approaches differ in
whether preferences are specified explicitly or adapted from objective
statistics. In differential adversarial tracking, the discriminator
instead learns this aggregation from the joint residual distribution.

\textbf{Monotone and Lipschitz neural networks.} Constrained monotone
networks use nonnegative weights and suitable activation functions to
preserve componentwise input order \cite{runje2023constrained}. This
property depends on what the input coordinates mean; it must be imposed
on physical error magnitudes rather than arbitrary signed state features.
Gradient penalties and spectral normalization control the sensitivity of
adversarial critics \cite{gulrajani2017improved,miyato2018spectral}.
Algebraic constructions also provide Lipschitz bounds through diagonal
matrix constraints \cite{araujo2023unified}. MIRA uses the positive linear
construction from this framework within a monotone discriminator. The
underlying network constructions are prior work; our formulation applies
their joint constraints to physical-error adversarial motion rewards.

\section{Background}
\subsection{Reference-Conditioned Motion Tracking}
The policy tracks a synchronized reference trajectory while acting in
a physics simulator. Let $s_t$ be the simulated state, $\hat{s}_t$ its
reference, and $\phi_i(s)$ the $i$-th physical quantity of interest.

\subsection{Handcrafted Objective Scalarization}
Traditional methods partition the tracking quantities into $G$ objective
groups and assign a separate weight and error scale to each group. A
representative handcrafted reward is
\begin{equation}
r_t^{\mathrm{hand}}
=\sum_{g=1}^{G} w_g
\exp\!\Bigl[-\alpha_g
\bigl\|\phi_g(\hat{s}_t)-\phi_g(s_t)\bigr\|_2^2\Bigr],
\label{eq:hand}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_g$ extracts the quantities in group $g$, and $w_g$ and
$\alpha_g$ specify its weight and scale before training
\cite{peng2018deepmimic}. A learned reward instead estimates the
aggregation from the errors observed during training.

\subsection{Differential Adversarial Objectives}
\label{sec:learned}

ADD~\cite{zhang2025add} uses the signed differential between
synchronized reference and policy features. It labels zero as the ideal
differential and trains a discriminator to separate it from
differentials encountered by the policy. Higher discriminator scores
produce higher imitation rewards, allowing the model to learn the
relative importance of the tracking objectives.

\section{MIRA: Monotonic Imitation Rewards via Adversarial Learning}
\label{sec:mira}

\subsection{Physical Error Representation}
\label{sec:physical_errors}

MIRA's monotone discriminator requires each input component to have a clear
error interpretation: a smaller value indicates that the corresponding
physical quantity is closer to the reference state. However, directly
computing the difference between the current and reference states does not satisfy
this requirement. For example, a velocity difference may be positive or
negative, and a change from $+1$ to $-1$ does not indicate improved
tracking. Rotations are commonly represented by quaternions or 6D
representations, and directly subtracting the representation vectors does
not accurately measure the actual orientation deviation. We therefore
convert raw state differences into nonnegative physical errors, so that
the magnitude of each component reflects the corresponding tracking error.

Let $s_t$ and $\hat{s}_t$ denote the robot's current state and the
synchronized reference state, respectively. For positions and rotations,
we use Euclidean distances and geodesic angles, respectively, to convert
the deviations of the root and each body part into scalar errors. For
joint velocity, root linear velocity, and root angular velocity, we use
componentwise absolute differences to retain independent errors in each
direction.

We thus construct the physical error vector:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf e_t = \bigl[
&e_{\mathrm{rp}}, e_{\mathrm{rr}},
\mathbf e_{\mathrm{bp}}, \mathbf e_{\mathrm{br}},
\mathbf e_{\mathrm{dv}}, \mathbf e_{\mathrm{rv}},
\mathbf e_{\mathrm{rav}}
\bigr]
\in \mathbb R_{\geq 0}^{d},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:physical_errors}
\end{equation}
where the seven error groups represent root position, root rotation, body
position, body rotation, joint velocity, root linear velocity, and root
angular velocity errors, respectively. The dimension of each group is
determined by the robot's body structure and joint configuration. The
total dimension is $d=\sum_{g=1}^{G}d_g$, where $G=7$.

Different physical errors have different units and numerical ranges. For
example, position is measured in meters, rotation in radians, and linear
and angular velocities in meters per second and radians per second,
respectively. If these errors are fed directly into the discriminator,
errors with larger numerical scales may dominate network learning, while
the influence of other errors is weakened. To address this, during early
training we collect the historical mean of each group's tracking metric
as the normalization scale for the corresponding error group, and fix
these scales after the statistics collection phase. All errors within
the same group share this scale.

In addition, error groups such as body position and joint velocity
typically contain more error terms, whereas root position and rotation
each contain only one. Without accounting for differences in dimension,
high-dimensional error groups may occupy a larger share of the input
simply because they contain more error terms, biasing discriminator
learning toward high-dimensional body and joint errors while overlooking
the influence of low-dimensional errors such as those of the root. We
therefore further introduce group-dimension normalization:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf z_t =
\operatorname{concat}_{g=1}^{G}
\left(
\frac{\mathbf e_t^{(g)}}
{\max(\sigma_g,\epsilon)\sqrt{d_g}}
\right),
\label{eq:physical_normalization}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf e_t^{(g)}$ is the physical error vector of group $g$,
$\sigma_g$ is the historical mean of the corresponding tracking metric,
$d_g$ is the number of error terms in the group, and $\epsilon$ is a
numerical stability constant. This normalization can prevent the
discriminator from overemphasizing errors with larger numerical
magnitudes or more components.

\subsection{Adversarial Reward Learning}
\label{sec:reward}

In reinforcement learning, a motion-tracking policy learns the reference
motion by maximizing an imitation reward. Traditional methods typically
combine tracking rewards for position, orientation, and velocity through
a weighted sum, relying on manually specified and tuned weights. MIRA
therefore learns an imitation reward from the normalized physical errors
$\mathbf z_t$ through adversarial learning and introduces monotonicity
constraints so that reducing errors cannot decrease the reward.

The discriminator maps the normalized physical error $\mathbf z$ to a
scalar through a monotone neural network $E_\theta$. The score is defined
as:
\begin{equation}
s_\theta(\mathbf z)
=b-\left[E_\theta(\mathbf z)-E_\theta(\mathbf 0)\right],
\label{eq:mira_score}
\end{equation}
where $b$ is a learnable scalar, and the score at zero error is
$s_\theta(\mathbf 0)=b$. In practice, the zero-error vector is fed into
the network together with the error inputs of the current batch to
compute $E_\theta(\mathbf 0)$, which is shared by all samples in that
batch. Since $E_\theta$ is monotone, increasing one error while
holding the others fixed cannot increase the discriminator score.

The discriminator is trained using binary cross-entropy. The ideal state
in which all physical errors are zero, $\mathbf z^+=\mathbf 0$, serves
as the positive sample, while actual tracking errors $\mathbf z^-$ from
the current policy and experience replay serve as negative samples. The
classification loss is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_D ={}&
\frac12\operatorname{softplus}
\left[-s_\theta(\mathbf 0)\right] \\
&+\frac12\mathbb E_{\mathbf z^-\sim q_{-}}
\left[\operatorname{softplus}
\left(s_\theta(\mathbf z^-)\right)\right],
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:mira_loss}
\end{equation}
where $q_{-}$ denotes the negative-sample distribution. The first term of
the loss encourages the discriminator to increase the score for zero
error, while the second encourages it to decrease scores for actual
tracking errors. By minimizing this loss, the discriminator learns to
distinguish zero error from actual tracking errors and to combine the
physical errors for scoring.

The discriminator score is converted into an imitation reward using the
same reward mapping as ADD:
\begin{equation}
r_t=\min\left\{\operatorname{softplus}\left(s_\theta(\mathbf z_t)\right),
              -\log(10^{-4})\right\}.
\label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
The reward cannot decrease as the score increases. The cap is the
numerical safeguard used in the ADD reward implementation. During training, the
discriminator updates its parameters using current samples and experience
replay, while the policy is optimized through PPO using the learned
reward. The two are updated alternately.

\subsection{Constrained Monotone Discriminator}
\label{sec:critic}

The discriminator must not only distinguish zero error from actual
tracking errors, but also provide distinguishable rewards for different
levels of tracking quality. If the discriminator is overly sensitive to input
errors, even small errors may cause its score to drop sharply, leading
to nearly zero rewards for different degrees of tracking error. ADD uses
a gradient penalty to constrain discriminator sensitivity, but a penalty
coefficient that is too small provides insufficient constraint, while
one that is too large may make the discriminator overly smooth and
weaken its ability to distinguish different errors. To avoid dependence
on this penalty coefficient, MIRA directly constrains discriminator
sensitivity through its network architecture while ensuring that its
score is monotone with respect to physical errors.

Specifically, MIRA combines nonnegative weights, monotone activation
functions, and learnable weight scaling so that $E_\theta$ satisfies
both monotonicity and a $1$-Lipschitz constraint.

To guarantee monotonicity, we take the elementwise absolute value of the
raw weight matrix $V$ of each linear layer to obtain the nonnegative
matrix $P=|V|$. Because the weights are nonnegative, increasing any input
error cannot decrease the output of the linear layer. Combined with
monotone activation functions, this property propagates from layer to
layer, thereby guaranteeing the monotonicity of the entire network
$E_\theta$.

The network uses MixedELU activation functions, whose branches are all
monotonically nondecreasing with derivatives no greater than $1$,
thereby preserving monotonicity across network layers
\cite{runje2023constrained}.

To satisfy the $1$-Lipschitz constraint, we scale the weight matrix
columnwise. The scaling coefficients are controlled by learnable
parameters, allowing the discriminator to adjust the contributions of
different physical errors to its score while bounding the spectral norm
of each linear layer by $1$.

Let $P\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}$ be a nonnegative weight matrix, where
$m$ and $n$ are the output and input dimensions, respectively. Introduce
learnable parameters $\boldsymbol\alpha\in\mathbb R^n$ and let
$\mathbf q=\exp(\boldsymbol\alpha)>0$. The scaling coefficients are
computed from the entire weight matrix:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf t=\frac{P^\top P\mathbf q}{\mathbf q},
\label{eq:diagonal_coefficients}
\end{equation}
where division is elementwise. Since $P\geq0$ and $\mathbf q>0$,
$t_i$ is positive for every nonzero weight column.

Using $\mathbf t$, we scale the weight matrix columnwise to obtain the
effective weight matrix used in the forward computation:
\begin{equation}
W=P\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf t^{-1/2}).
\label{eq:diagonal_weights}
\end{equation}
Zero weight columns remain zero. This scaling satisfies the matrix
constraints for Lipschitz networks \cite{araujo2023unified}, guaranteeing:
\begin{equation}
W\geq0,\qquad \|W\|_2\leq1.
\label{eq:diagonal_bound}
\end{equation}
The following guarantee holds for each fixed set of discriminator weights
and normalization scales.

\begin{theorem}[Monotone imitation reward]
\label{thm:reward}
Let $r$ be the reward in Eq.~\eqref{eq:reward}. For normalized physical
errors $\mathbf z,\mathbf z'\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^{d}$,
\begin{align}
\mathbf z\leq\mathbf z'&\ \Longrightarrow\ r(\mathbf z)\geq r(\mathbf z'),\notag\\
|r(\mathbf z)-r(\mathbf z')|&\leq\|\mathbf z-\mathbf z'\|_2 .
\end{align}
The order is componentwise. In particular, zero error maximizes the reward.
\end{theorem}
Appendix~\ref{app:proof} proves the result. These properties govern how the
reward responds to error changes; they place no bound on policy sensitivity.

\section{Conclusion}
MIRA learns one adversarial imitation reward from componentwise physical
tracking errors. Nonnegative weights and monotone activations provide a
consistent error-improvement ordering, while learned diagonal
normalization bounds sensitivity in normalized error coordinates without
a gradient penalty. Across six motions, MIRA's clearest empirical gains
are in tracking the root, with smaller errors in articulated pose as well.
The joint-velocity comparison identifies a remaining trade-off. In the
three contact motions, MIRA tracks the timing of reference speed changes
more closely and reduces foot slip during stance. The Backflip ablations
separate the effects of monotonicity, group dimension scaling, and
diagonal normalization. Further independent training seeds are needed to
establish the generality of these results.
