\title{\LARGE \bf MIRA: Monotonic Imitation Rewards via Adversarial Learning}
\author{Anonymous Authors}

\begin{abstract}
Humanoid motion imitation requires jointly considering multiple tracking
objectives, including position, orientation, and velocity. Conventional
reward functions rely on manually designed weights and scales, often
requiring repeated tuning for different motions. This paper presents MIRA,
a monotonic imitation reward method based on adversarial learning. MIRA
represents deviations between the current state and a synchronized reference
state as nonnegative physical errors. It constructs a monotone discriminator
using nonnegative weights and learnable diagonal normalization to adaptively
learn trade-offs among different errors while ensuring that reducing errors
cannot decrease the reward. Experimental results show that MIRA achieves
accurate and stable motion tracking across different motions with a unified
reward configuration.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Physics-based humanoid motion imitation requires the controller to
reproduce a prescribed reference motion under dynamic constraints,
actuator limits, and complex contact conditions. Whether an imitation
succeeds is not a single geometric question. The root must translate and
turn as the reference does, the articulated body must maintain consistent
poses, joint and root velocities must stay synchronized with the reference
motion, and contacts must occur at the right moments. Because these
objectives have different physical units, numerical ranges, and dynamical
characteristics, and are coupled through the robot dynamics, motion
imitation is inherently a multi-objective control problem.

Traditional methods transform several tracking errors separately and
combine them into a scalar reward through manually chosen weights and
scales. These coefficients determine both the relative importance and the
numerical response of the tracking objectives. Because typical errors and
dynamical difficulty change with the motion, a coefficient set that serves
one skill need not serve another; in practice, reward design is often
re-tuned whenever the reference motion changes.

Adversarial imitation offers a way to reduce manual reward design.
Instead of declaring in advance how many meters of root-position error
are worth how many radians of rotation error, a discriminator learns a
unified imitation signal directly from data. AMP and related methods
learn rewards that describe motion style and plausibility by
discriminating whether short state transitions come from the reference
distribution \cite{peng2021amp}. For motion tracking that must strictly
follow a synchronized reference trajectory, ADD further feeds the
reference--policy state differential to the discriminator and asks
whether it is close to the ideal zero differential \cite{zhang2025add}.
The aggregation among tracking objectives can then be learned by the
discriminator instead of specifying component-wise weights and scales by
hand.

Differential classification leaves two properties unspecified. First,
separating the zero differential from policy residuals does not require a
discriminator to rank two nonzero residuals consistently with physical
tracking improvement. For example, reducing a rotation-representation
coordinate difference need not reduce the actual angular error. Second,
classification does not bound how rapidly a score changes near the policy's
current states. A useful tracking reward therefore requires both an input
ordering grounded in physical errors and control over its sensitivity.

MIRA builds these properties into a single discriminator. A synchronized
state pair is converted into $64$ nonnegative error components: Euclidean
position distances, geodesic rotation angles, and absolute velocity gaps.
Individual non-root bodies and joint-velocity components remain separate.
Seven shared group scales account for physical units, and dimension
normalization controls the contribution of group size to the input norm.
Nonnegative weights and monotone activations make the energy nondecreasing
in every error component. A positive, learnable diagonal normalization
bounds each linear map's spectral norm without a gradient penalty
\cite{araujo2023unified}. Anchoring the energy at zero error then produces
a score that cannot decrease when all tracking errors improve.

These guarantees apply in normalized physical-error coordinates. They do
not specify how to trade a smaller body error against a larger root error,
guarantee a nonzero response to every joint, or imply that PPO finds a
globally optimal controller. We retain the original adversarial
classification objective and reward mapping so that the experiments can
examine the resulting tracking behavior directly.

Our contributions are threefold. First, we formulate an adversarial
tracking reward on componentwise physical errors with an explicit
improvement ordering. Second, we combine constrained monotone networks
\cite{runje2023constrained} with positive diagonal normalization to obtain
one monotone, sensitivity-bounded reward model trained without a gradient
penalty. Third, we evaluate a shared configuration on six humanoid skills
and ablate the representation/monotonicity component and the normalization
constraint, reporting both tracking errors and motion completion.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Physics-based motion tracking and learned imitation objectives.}
DeepMimic combines pose, velocity, root, and end-effector tracking terms
for physics-based skills \cite{peng2018deepmimic}; subsequent work
improves scalability and motion coverage through architectures,
representations, and data \cite{luo2023phc,tessler2023maskedmimic}.
Adversarial imitation provides a learned alternative to a manually
written reward: GAIL matches expert and policy occupancies
\cite{ho2016gail}, while AMP learns a motion prior by discriminating
short state transitions \cite{peng2021amp}. ADD addresses
reference-conditioned tracking by presenting the reference--policy
differential to an adversarial discriminator and learning a nonlinear
aggregation of tracking objectives without component-wise reward weights
\cite{zhang2025add}.

\textbf{Multi-objective learning and objective aggregation.} Competing
objectives are commonly combined by weighted scalarization, Pareto
optimization, or adaptive loss weighting
\cite{sener2018multitask,kendall2018multi}. These approaches differ in
whether preferences are specified explicitly or adapted from objective
statistics. In differential adversarial tracking, the discriminator
instead learns this aggregation from the joint residual distribution.

\textbf{Monotone and Lipschitz neural networks.} Constrained monotone
networks use nonnegative weights and suitable activation functions to
preserve componentwise input order \cite{runje2023constrained}. This
property depends on what the input coordinates mean; it must be imposed
on physical error magnitudes rather than arbitrary signed state features.
Gradient penalties and spectral normalization control the sensitivity of
adversarial critics \cite{gulrajani2017improved,miyato2018spectral}.
Algebraic constructions also provide Lipschitz bounds through diagonal
matrix constraints \cite{araujo2023unified}. MIRA uses the positive linear
construction from this framework within a monotone discriminator. The
underlying network constructions are prior work; our formulation applies
their joint constraints to physical-error adversarial motion rewards.

\section{Background}
\subsection{Reference-Conditioned Motion Tracking}
In reference-conditioned humanoid motion imitation, the controller must
keep the executed motion continuously aligned with a time-synchronized
reference trajectory. Let $s_t$ and $\hat{s}_t$ denote the current state
and the reference state at time $t$. Tracking quality is jointly measured
by several physical quantities, including root position and orientation,
articulated body pose, and motion velocities. Let $\phi_i(s_t)$ extract
the $i$-th tracking quantity from the current state and let
$\phi_i(\hat{s}_t)$ denote its reference counterpart. These quantities
may have different dimensions and physical units---positions are measured
in meters, while rotations and velocities have their own representations.

\subsection{Handcrafted Objective Scalarization}
Traditional methods partition the tracking quantities into $G$ objective
groups and assign a separate weight and error scale to each group. A
representative handcrafted reward is
\begin{equation}
r_t^{\mathrm{hand}}
=\sum_{g=1}^{G} w_g
\exp\!\Bigl[-\alpha_g
\bigl\|\phi_g(\hat{s}_t)-\phi_g(s_t)\bigr\|_2^2\Bigr],
\label{eq:hand}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_g$ extracts the tracking quantities in group $g$, while $w_g$
and $\alpha_g$ set its relative importance and error scale. DeepMimic is a
representative instance of this class: its tracking terms use weights and
error scales specified before training and held fixed throughout
\cite{peng2018deepmimic}. This is a fixed scalarization of the tracking
objectives: the trade-off among groups is fixed through constant
coefficients chosen before training starts.
Because typical error magnitudes and dynamical characteristics differ
across motions, one coefficient set need not serve all motions. Learned
scalarization instead leaves the trade-off unspecified in advance and
learns the aggregation among objectives from the error distribution that
actually occurs during training.

\subsection{Differential Adversarial Objectives}
\label{sec:learned}

Differential adversarial learning provides such a learned aggregation
\cite{zhang2025add}. Consider $n$ non-negative objective losses
$l^1,\ldots,l^n$ collected into a differential vector
$\Delta=(l^1,\ldots,l^n)$, where each entry records the gap between the
current solution and the ideal on one objective. When all objectives are
simultaneously ideal, $\Delta=0$. A discriminator is trained to assign a
high score to the ideal zero differential and lower scores to the nonzero
differentials produced by the current policy. Because it receives all
objective differentials jointly, the discriminator can learn a nonlinear
aggregation of the objectives without fixed per-objective weights. Its
sensitivity to individual objectives may vary across the residual space
and throughout training, allowing the learned aggregation to adapt to the
differential distribution encountered during optimization.

In synchronized motion tracking, the differential can be formed directly
from the feature residual between the current and reference states rather
than from one scalar loss per objective. Perfect tracking again corresponds
to the unique zero differential, while nonzero differentials preserve the
magnitude, direction, and correlations of the tracking errors. The
discriminator score can therefore be used to construct a policy reward
that increases as the current differential is judged more consistent with
perfect tracking.

\section{MIRA: Monotonic Imitation Rewards via Adversarial Learning}
\label{sec:mira}

\subsection{Physical Error Representation}
\label{sec:physical_errors}

Physics-based motion imitation requires simultaneously tracking the robot's
position, orientation, and motion velocities. However, the coordinatewise
ordering of raw state residuals does not consistently represent physical
tracking improvement. For example, position and
velocity differences are signed, although deviations in either direction
indicate tracking errors. Rotations are commonly represented by quaternions
or 6D representations, whose Euclidean distances do not equal the actual
rotation angles. We therefore convert state discrepancies into nonnegative
errors with clear physical meanings.

Let $s_t$ and $\hat{s}_t$ denote the robot's current state and the
synchronized reference state, respectively. Position errors are measured
using Euclidean distances, rotation errors using geodesic angles, and
velocity errors using componentwise absolute differences. Body positions
are computed relative to the root, whereas body rotations use world-frame
orientations. The position and rotation errors of individual body parts
are retained separately to capture tracking quality across different
parts of the body. We thus construct the physical error vector
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf e_t = \bigl[
&e_{\mathrm{rp}}, e_{\mathrm{rr}},
\mathbf e_{\mathrm{bp}}, \mathbf e_{\mathrm{br}},
\mathbf e_{\mathrm{dv}}, \mathbf e_{\mathrm{rv}},
\mathbf e_{\mathrm{rav}}
\bigr]
\in \mathbb R_{\geq 0}^{64},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:physical_errors}
\end{equation}
where the terms represent root position, root rotation, body position,
body rotation, joint velocity, root linear velocity, and root angular
velocity errors, respectively.
The seven groups contain $1$, $1$, $14$, $14$, $28$, $3$, and $3$
components. The body groups exclude the root to avoid duplicating the
separate root errors.

Because different physical errors have different numerical scales,
running statistics are used during early training to estimate scales
from the seven logged tracking metrics. Each scale is shared by the
retained components of its group and fixed after the statistics
collection phase. The normalized input is defined as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf z_t =
\operatorname{concat}_{g=1}^{G}
\left(
\frac{\mathbf e_t^{(g)}}
{\max(\sigma_g,\epsilon)\sqrt{d_g}}
\right),
\label{eq:physical_normalization}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf e_t^{(g)}$ is the physical error vector for group $g$,
$\sigma_g$ is the historical mean of its corresponding tracking metric,
$d_g$ is the number of
components in the group, and $\epsilon$ is a numerical stability constant.
Dimension normalization controls group-size-dependent growth of the input
norm; equally scaled component errors yield the same group norm regardless
of the number of components. Since all errors are nonnegative and normalization uses
positive scaling, the resulting input $\mathbf z_t$ remains nonnegative
and preserves the monotonic ordering of each error component.

\subsection{Adversarial Reward Learning}
\label{sec:reward}

To learn how different physical errors should be evaluated jointly, MIRA
uses a monotone discriminator to construct a unified imitation reward
through adversarial learning, reducing reliance on manually designed
reward weights.

The discriminator maps the normalized physical error $\mathbf z$ to a
scalar through a monotone neural network $E_\theta$. Its score is defined
as
\begin{equation}
s_\theta(\mathbf z)
=b-\left[E_\theta(\mathbf z)-E_\theta(\mathbf 0)\right],
\label{eq:mira_score}
\end{equation}
where $b$ is a learnable scalar. At zero error, the score is
$s_\theta(\mathbf 0)=b$. Since $E_\theta$ is monotone, increasing physical
error components cannot decrease $E_\theta(\mathbf z)$ and therefore
cannot increase the discriminator score $s_\theta(\mathbf z)$.

The discriminator is trained using binary cross-entropy. The zero-error
vector $\mathbf z^+=\mathbf 0$ serves as the positive sample, while errors
$\mathbf z^-$ from the current policy and experience replay serve as
negative samples. The classification loss is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_D ={}&
\frac12\operatorname{softplus}
\left[-s_\theta(\mathbf 0)\right] \\
&+\frac12\mathbb E_{\mathbf z^-\sim q_{-}}
\left[\operatorname{softplus}
\left(s_\theta(\mathbf z^-)\right)\right],
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:mira_loss}
\end{equation}
where $q_{-}$ denotes the negative-sample distribution. This objective
encourages higher scores for zero error and lower scores for errors
produced by the policy, thereby learning a joint evaluation of different
tracking errors. Output-layer weight regularization is also retained
during training.

The discriminator score is converted into an imitation reward using the
same reward mapping as ADD:
\begin{equation}
r_t=\operatorname{softplus}\left(s_\theta(\mathbf z_t)\right).
\label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
As in ADD, the implementation caps this mapping using
$-\log\!\left(\max\{1-\operatorname{sigmoid}(s),10^{-4}\}\right)$;
it equals softplus before the cap.
The capped mapping is nondecreasing, so increasing the discriminator score
cannot lower the imitation reward. During training, the
discriminator is updated using current samples and experience replay,
while the policy is optimized with PPO using the learned reward. The
discriminator and policy are updated alternately.

\subsection{Constrained Monotone Discriminator}
\label{sec:critic}

MIRA combines nonnegative weights, monotone activation functions, and
learnable weight scaling so that $E_\theta$ satisfies both monotonicity
and a $1$-Lipschitz constraint.

To guarantee monotonicity, the raw weight matrix $V$ of each linear layer
is transformed elementwise into the nonnegative matrix $P=|V|$. For
nonnegative physical error inputs $\mathbf z\geq0$, nonnegative weights
ensure that increasing any error component cannot decrease the outputs of
a linear layer. The network uses MixedELU activations, whose branches are
all monotonically nondecreasing with derivatives bounded above by $1$,
preserving monotonicity across network layers
\cite{runje2023constrained}.

To satisfy the $1$-Lipschitz constraint, we scale the weight matrix
columnwise. The scaling factors are controlled by learnable parameters,
allowing the discriminator to flexibly balance the contributions of
different physical errors to its score while bounding the spectral norm
of each linear layer by $1$.

Specifically, let $P\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}$ be a nonnegative weight
matrix, where $m$ and $n$ are the output and input dimensions,
respectively. Introduce learnable parameters
$\boldsymbol\alpha\in\mathbb R^n$ and let
$\mathbf q=\exp(\boldsymbol\alpha)>0$. The scaling coefficients are
computed from the entire weight matrix as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf t=\frac{P^\top P\mathbf q}{\mathbf q},
\label{eq:diagonal_coefficients}
\end{equation}
where division is elementwise. Since $P\geq0$, every entry of $P^\top P$
is nonnegative. Together with $\mathbf q>0$, this ensures that $t_i>0$
for every nonzero weight column.

Using $\mathbf t$, we scale the weight matrix columnwise to obtain the
effective matrix used in the forward computation:
\begin{equation}
W=P\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf t^{-1/2}).
\label{eq:diagonal_weights}
\end{equation}
Zero weight columns remain zero.
This scaling satisfies the matrix constraint for Lipschitz networks
\cite{araujo2023unified}, guaranteeing
\begin{equation}
W\geq0,\qquad \|W\|_2\leq1.
\label{eq:diagonal_bound}
\end{equation}
Thus, every linear layer maintains nonnegative weights and a Lipschitz
constant no greater than $1$ throughout training. Combined with monotone,
$1$-Lipschitz activation functions, the complete network $E_\theta$ and
its associated imitation reward satisfy monotonicity and a $1$-Lipschitz
constraint in the normalized physical error space.

\section{Conclusion}
MIRA learns one adversarial imitation reward from componentwise physical
tracking errors. Nonnegative weights and monotone activations provide a
consistent error-improvement ordering, while learned diagonal
normalization bounds sensitivity in normalized error coordinates without
a gradient penalty. The six-skill seed-0 comparison with AMP,
DeepMimic, and ADD reports tracking accuracy under the listed training
budgets. Backflip ablations associate both model components
with improved tracking; removing diagonal normalization also reduces
motion completion. Further independent training seeds
and evaluation across additional motions are needed to establish the
generality of these empirical gains; monotonicity and bounded sensitivity
alone do not guarantee a preferred trade-off among competing errors.
